🎯 The result 1 / 5

Theorem 2.

For \(1 \le m \le 18\) the values \(\mathrm{T}(m)\), \(S(m)\) and, for \(m \ge 2\), \(M(m)\) in Table 1 are exact, and the extremizers of \(\mathrm{T}(m)\) are exactly the multigraphs of Table 2, up to isomorphism. The extremizers of \(S(m)\) and \(M(m)\) are listed in the data files.

💬 In plain words 2 / 5

Take $m$ edges and build any connected multigraph you like, with as many vertices as you want: how many spanning trees can it have at most? For $m \le 18$ the paper gives the exact answer, $\mathrm{T}(m) = 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 16, 24, 45, \dots, 12584$, and every multigraph that attains it.

Why it is true: the proof is a computation that can be re-run. For each $m$ and each of the three classes (all, simple, non-simple) a certificate splits the class into pieces and either rules a piece out with a bound proved in §3 or lists its graphs; a checker written with the Python standard library only recomputes every bound, every determinant and the completeness of every list by its own counting, without nauty. It accepted all $53$ certificates.

What the paper claims: the values and the complete lists of extremizers for $m \le 18$; the trust base is the checker's code and the rules of §3. The lab below recomputes $\tau$ of every listed extremizer in your browser.

🛠️ How it is proved 3 / 5

What the paper contributes

$\mathrm{T}(m)$ is the largest number of spanning trees of a connected loopless multigraph with $m$ edges, the number of vertices being free: the first question of Problem 2 of Kenyon (1996). The paper is a data note:

  • Exact values and all extremizers for $m\le 22$, with the simple maximum $S(m)$ and the non-simple maximum $M(m)$ (Table 1, Table 2). The sequence begins $1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 16, 24, 45, 81, 135, \dots$ and ends with $\mathrm{T}(22)=130691$, attained only by the circulant $C_{11}(1,3)$.
  • Certified for $m\le 18$ (Theorem 2, computer-assisted): $53$ certificates accepted by a stand-alone checker that uses only the Python standard library and checks the completeness of every enumeration by its own counting, without nauty. Search only for $19\le m\le 22$ (Computational result 3): the same proved rules, skeleton lists from nauty, no certificate.
  • The pruning rules, each with a written proof (Lemmas 5–11, Corollary 12): monotonicity, cut vertices, vertices of degree two, counting bounds, bounds on a fixed skeleton, and two lemmas on multiple edges, from Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem, effective resistance and Rayleigh monotonicity.
  • Observations read off the tables (Remark 4): from $m=8$ on every extremizer is simple with degrees $3$ and $4$; a multiple edge is needed only for $m=2,4$.

The rules rest on three classical facts (Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem, effective resistance, Rayleigh monotonicity); the bound $\tau(G)^2\,8^m<27^m$ and the answer to Kenyon's second question are Majchrzak's (2026); the classical extremal problem with $n$ fixed is Kelmans–Chelnokov's, Shier's, Cheng's and Petingi–Boesch–Suffel's. It rests on:

  • Electrical networks and the matrix-tree theorem (Kirchhoff; Rayleigh) — (K1) the matrix-tree theorem for multigraphs, (K2) the share of spanning trees through a copy is the effective resistance of its ends, (K3) Rayleigh monotonicity: every pruning rule is proved from these three facts
  • Graph enumeration (nauty; generating functions for labelled graphs) — the search lists skeletons with nauty’s geng and multig; the checker proves each list complete by orbit sums Σ n!/|Aut| against labelled counts from generating functions (OEIS A001187, A013922), without nauty
  • Kenyon’s Problem 2 and Majchrzak’s sharp bound — the question (Kenyon 1996) and the bound τ(G)²8^m < 27^m with optimal base √(27/8) (Majchrzak 2026), against which the growth of T(m) is compared
