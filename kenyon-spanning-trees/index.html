Maximum number of spanning trees of a multigraph with m edges: exact values for m ≤ 22

🏷️ Extremal graph theory · spanning trees of multigraphs · computer-assisted enumeration with certificates

Carles Marín

At a glance

What the paper contributes

$\mathrm{T}(m)$ is the largest number of spanning trees of a connected loopless multigraph with $m$ edges, the number of vertices being free: the first question of Problem 2 of Kenyon (1996). The paper is a data note:

  • Exact values and all extremizers for $m\le 22$, with the simple maximum $S(m)$ and the non-simple maximum $M(m)$ (Table 1, Table 2). The sequence begins $1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 16, 24, 45, 81, 135, \dots$ and ends with $\mathrm{T}(22)=130691$, attained only by the circulant $C_{11}(1,3)$.
  • Certified for $m\le 18$ (Theorem 2, computer-assisted): $53$ certificates accepted by a stand-alone checker that uses only the Python standard library and checks the completeness of every enumeration by its own counting, without nauty. Search only for $19\le m\le 22$ (Computational result 3): the same proved rules, skeleton lists from nauty, no certificate.
  • The pruning rules, each with a written proof (Lemmas 5–11, Corollary 12): monotonicity, cut vertices, vertices of degree two, counting bounds, bounds on a fixed skeleton, and two lemmas on multiple edges, from Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem, effective resistance and Rayleigh monotonicity.
  • Observations read off the tables (Remark 4): from $m=8$ on every extremizer is simple with degrees $3$ and $4$; a multiple edge is needed only for $m=2,4$.

The rules rest on three classical facts (Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem, effective resistance, Rayleigh monotonicity); the bound $\tau(G)^2\,8^m<27^m$ and the answer to Kenyon's second question are Majchrzak's (2026); the classical extremal problem with $n$ fixed is Kelmans–Chelnokov's, Shier's, Cheng's and Petingi–Boesch–Suffel's. The paper reports that the sequence was not in the OEIS on 25 November 2026. The table below uses the paper's colour code.

📐 Setting (copied from the paper: §1, Definition 1, and §3)

All multigraphs are finite and loopless; parallel edges are distinct edges. For a multigraph \(G\) let \(\tau (G)\) be its number of spanning trees, so \(\tau (G) = 0\) if \(G\) is disconnected. (§1)

For \(m \ge 1\) let \(\mathrm{T}(m) = \max \tau (G)\) over connected loopless multigraphs with \(m\) edges and any number of vertices; \(S(m)\) is the same maximum over simple graphs and \(M(m)\) over multigraphs with at least one edge of multiplicity \(\ge 2\) (\(M(1)\) is undefined). An extremizer of \(\mathrm{T}(m)\) is a multigraph attaining it. (Definition 1)

Notation: \(R_{uv}(G)\) is the effective resistance between \(u\) and \(v\) with unit resistance on every copy. We use three classical facts (Kirchhoff): (K1) the matrix-tree theorem, valid for multigraphs; (K2) the fraction of spanning trees using a given copy \(f = uv\) is \(R_{uv}(G)\), so \(\tau (G - f) = \tau (G)(1 - R_{uv})\) and \(\tau (G + uv) = \tau (G)(1 + R_{uv})\); (K3) Rayleigh monotonicity: adding edges does not increase effective resistances. (§3)

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Take \(m\) edges and build any connected multigraph you like, with as many vertices as you want: how many spanning trees can it have at most? For \(m \le 18\) the paper gives the exact answer, \(\mathrm{T}(m) = 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 16, 24, 45, \dots , 12584\), and every multigraph that attains it.

Why it is true: the proof is a computation that can be re-run. For each \(m\) and each of the three classes (all, simple, non-simple) a certificate splits the class into pieces and either rules a piece out with a bound proved in §3 or lists its graphs; a checker written with the Python standard library only recomputes every bound, every determinant and the completeness of every list by its own counting, without nauty. It accepted all \(53\) certificates.

What the paper claims: the values and the complete lists of extremizers for \(m \le 18\); the trust base is the checker’s code and the rules of §3. The lab below recomputes \(\tau \) of every listed extremizer in your browser.

🧪 Lab: The extremal multigraphs in 3D: every extremizer of T(m), S(m), M(m) for m ≤ 22, τ recomputed in your browser

For the \(m\) and the class you choose (\(\mathrm{T}\): all loopless multigraphs; \(S\): simple graphs; \(M\): multigraphs with a multiple edge) the lab takes every extremizer listed in extremal_graphs.json of the Zenodo record and recomputes \(\tau \) by Kirchhoff’s theorem (K1): the determinant of the Laplacian (with multiplicities) without one row and column, by fraction-free elimination in exact integers (BigInt). It compares \(\tau \) with the file and with Table 1, checks the number of edges and the degrees, and computes for each edge the number of spanning trees through one copy, \(\tau (G)-\tau (G-f)\); by (K2) the share \((\tau (G)-\tau (G-f))/\tau (G)\) is the effective resistance of its ends, and the shares of all copies add up to \(n-1\) (each tree has \(n-1\) edges), which is checked exactly. The 3D figure (drag to turn it; the slider and ▶ run through \(m=1,\dots ,22\)) draws the extremizers with vertices coloured by degree and each edge copy as a curve, the copies of a multiple edge bowing apart in purple, the width of a copy growing with its share of spanning trees; the vertex positions come from a deterministic force-directed layout and carry no information. Next to it, the same graph flat (vertices labelled as in the file), and below the growth \(\mathrm{T}(m)^{1/m}\) against Majchrzak’s base \(\sqrt{27/8}\) and Table 1 in the paper’s colours (blue certified, vermilion search only, purple a multiple edge).

  1. A multiple edge is needed to attain \(\mathrm{T}(m)\) only for \(m = 2\) and \(m = 4\); for \(m = 3, 5, 7\) a simple and a non-simple extremizer tie. Hence \(S(m) = \mathrm{T}(m)\) for every \(m \le 22\) except \(m = 2, 4\).

  2. For \(8 \le m \le 22\) every extremizer is simple with all degrees \(3\) or \(4\), and it is unique except at \(m = 13\) (two). Identified up to isomorphism: \(K_4\) (\(m=6\)), \(K_{3,3}\) (\(9\)), \(K_{3,4}\) (\(12\)), \(K_{4,4}\) minus an edge (\(15\)), \(K_{4,4} \cong C_8(1,3)\) (\(16\)), the circulants \(C_{10}(1,4)\) (\(20\)) and \(C_{11}(1,3)\) (\(22\)). The extremizer for \(m = 14\) is bipartite; those for \(m = 13, 17, 18, 19, 21\) are not.

  3. \(\mathrm{T}\) is strictly increasing, \(\mathrm{T}(m)^{1/m}\) decreases only from \(m = 6\) to \(m = 7\) (\(1.5874 \to 1.5746\)), and \(\mathrm{T}(22)^{1/22} = 1.7083\), below \(\sqrt{27/8} = 1.8371\).

  4. \(M(m)/S(m) \in [0.81, 0.94]\) for \(6 \le m \le 22\), \(m \ne 7\) (at \(m = 7\) they tie at \(24\)), and every extremizer of \(M(m)\) with \(4 \le m \le 22\) has maximal multiplicity exactly \(2\); this follows from the table by Corollary 12.

Majchrzak  [ 5 , § 9 ] reports, from an exhaustive computation over multigraphs with at most \(14\) edges, that the maximum of \(\tau ^{1/m}\) is \(1.667031\), at \(\tau = 1280\), \(m = 14\); this agrees with \(\mathrm{T}(14) = 1280\) and with Table 1, where \(\mathrm{T}(m)^{1/m}\) is largest at \(m = 14\) among \(m \le 14\).

Lemma 6 cut vertex
#

Uses

  • Lemma 5
  • Lemma 5
📕 p. 4📍 map❝ cite⚠ report

If \(G\) has a cut vertex, \(G = G_1 \cup _v G_2\) with \(m_1 + m_2 = m\), \(m_i \ge 1\), and \(\tau (G) = \tau (G_1)\tau (G_2) \le \mathrm{T}(m_1)\mathrm{T}(m_2)\). Moreover, for \(m \ge 2\) (multigraphs), \(m \ge 3\) (simple graphs) and in the class of non-simple multigraphs such a \(G\) is never extremal: there is \(G'\) in the same class with \(m\) edges and \(\tau (G') {\gt} \tau (G)\).

Trust base: ⚠️ not checked. Written proof; not machine-checked. Control 7 of the paper checks the construction on all 229 089 non-simple multigraphs with a cut vertex (n ≤ 6, m ≤ 8); Control 5 on every multigraph with m ≤ 12, n ≤ 7

Proof: in this note·Covers: not formalized·Cite: Lemma 6, p. 4, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24561280

🆕 Classical and new: the paper proves it from (K2) and the series law for effective resistance; no novelty claimed.

↳ Used in: Theorem 2, 3, Corollary 12

Proof ▼

A spanning tree of \(G\) is the union of one of \(G_1\) and one of \(G_2\). For the second claim: if \(G\) has a bridge \(b\), contract it (\(\tau \) unchanged, \(m-1\) edges, no loop) and either duplicate an edge (multigraphs) or subdivide an edge of a cycle, which adds \(\tau (G/b - f) \ge 1\) trees (simple graphs; if there is no cycle, \(G\) is a tree and \(C_m\) is better). If \(G\) has no bridge and \(v\) is a cut vertex, let \(u\) be a neighbour of \(v\) on one side, \(e\) a copy of \(uv\), \(w \ne v\) a vertex on the other side and \(H = G - e\), which is connected. Every \(u\)–\(w\) path in \(H\) passes through \(v\), so \(R_{uw}(H) = R_{uv}(H) + R_{vw}(H) {\gt} R_{uv}(H)\), and by (K2) \(\tau (H + uw) {\gt} \tau (H + uv) = \tau (G)\); \(H + uw\) is simple if \(G\) is. Non-simple class: contracting a bridge keeps every multiple edge and the added copy is one more; without bridges, every edge lies inside one side of \(v\), and if \(G\) has a single pair of multiplicity \(\ge 2\), of multiplicity exactly \(2\), we take \(u\) on the side that does not contain that pair, so that \(e\) is a simple edge and \(H + uw\) keeps the pair (otherwise removing one copy leaves a multiple edge anyway).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

A multigraph that falls apart into two pieces at a single vertex counts its spanning trees as a product, and it is never the best one: either contract a bridge and spend the saved edge elsewhere, or move one edge so that it jumps across the cut vertex, which by (K2) gains trees because the effective resistance across a cut vertex is a sum. So the search may assume two vertices joined by parallel edges, or a biconnected multigraph.

The class of non-simple multigraphs needs care (the move must keep a multiple edge), and the proof treats it separately; the paper checks the construction on all \(229\, 089\) non-simple multigraphs with a cut vertex with \(n \le 6\), \(m \le 8\) (Control 7).

Lemma 7 degree two
#
📕 p. 4📍 map❝ cite⚠ report

If \(G\) is biconnected with \(n \ge 3\) and has a vertex of degree \(2\), then \(\tau (G) \le \mathrm{T}(m-1) + \mathrm{T}(m-2)\), with \(\mathrm{T}(0) = 1\).

Trust base: ⚠️ not checked. Written proof (deletion–contraction); not machine-checked. The condition T(m−1) + T(m−2) < T(m) is checked on the table

Proof: in this note·Covers: not formalized·Cite: Lemma 7, p. 4, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24561280

🆕 Classical and new: the paper: deletion–contraction; no novelty claimed. It is applied only where T(m−1) + T(m−2) < T(m) on the computed values, «never assumed».

↳ Used in: Theorem 2, 3

Proof ▼

The vertex \(x\) has two distinct neighbours \(u, w\) (otherwise its neighbour is a cut vertex), and \(H = G - x\) is connected with \(m - 2\) edges. Either \(x\) is a leaf (\(2\tau (H)\) trees) or it uses both edges (\(\tau (H/uw)\) trees), so \(\tau (G) = \tau (H) + \tau (H + uw)\) by deletion–contraction.

Lemma 9 bounds on a fixed skeleton
#
📕 p. 4📍 map❝ cite⚠ report

Let \(S\) be a simple graph with \(e\) edges, \(G\) a multigraph on it with multiplicities \(m_f = 1 + x_f\) and \(r = m - e = \sum x_f\), and \(N_f\) the number of spanning trees of \(S\) through \(f\). Then

\[ \tau (G) \le \tau (S)\, P(n-1, r) \quad \text{and}\quad \tau (G) \le \tau (S) \prod _f \Bigl(1 + x_f \frac{N_f}{\tau (S)}\Bigr), \]

where \(P(k, r)\) is the largest product of \(k\) integers \(\ge 1\) with sum \(k + r\): writing \(k + r = qk + s\) with \(0 \le s {\lt} k\), \(P(k, r) = q^{k-s}(q+1)^{s}\).

Trust base: ⚠️ not checked. Written proof; not machine-checked. Control 4 of the paper compares the second bound with all biconnected non-simple multigraphs with n ≤ 7 for m = 6, 8, 10: never violated, attained on 3, 11 and 46 skeletons

Proof: in this note·Covers: not formalized·Cite: Lemma 9, p. 4, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24561280

🆕 Classical and new: the paper proves both bounds from (K2) and (K3) (the second telescopes); no novelty claimed.

↳ Used in: Theorem 2, 3

Proof ▼

First: \(\tau (G) = \sum _{T} \prod _{f \in T} m_f\) over trees \(T\) of \(S\), and each product has \(n-1\) factors whose excesses add up to at most \(r\). Second: add the extra copies of \(f\) one by one. By (K2) the \(j\)-th one multiplies \(\tau \) by \(1 + R\), where \(R\) is the current resistance of \(f\)’s endpoints; the network contains \(S\) (conductance \(\tau (S)/N_f\) between them) in parallel with \(j-1\) extra copies, so by (K3) \(R \le 1/(\tau (S)/N_f + j - 1)\), and the product telescopes. The maximum of the right-hand side over \(\sum x_f = r\) is attained by the greedy choice (each copy goes to an edge maximising \(N_f/(\tau (S) + x_f N_f)\), the marginal gain), since each factor is log-concave in \(x_f\); with a cap \(c\) on multiplicities, edges with \(x_f = c - 1\) are skipped.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Fix the underlying simple graph \(S\) (the skeleton) and ask how much spreading \(r\) extra parallel copies over its edges can raise the number of spanning trees. Two ceilings: each tree of \(S\) gains at most the largest product of \(n - 1\) multiplicities with the given total; and each extra copy of an edge multiplies \(\tau \) by at most \(1\) plus the current resistance between its ends, which Rayleigh monotonicity bounds by what \(S\) alone gives. If even the ceiling is below the best value known, the whole skeleton is discarded without expanding it.

This is the rule that keeps the search over multigraphs finite in practice; the paper compares the second bound with all biconnected non-simple multigraphs with \(n \le 7\) for \(m = 6, 8, 10\) (Control 4).

Lemma 10 multiplicity
#
📕 p. 5📍 map❝ cite⚠ report

If \(m \ge 4\) and \(G\) is biconnected with \(n \ge 3\) and an edge \(uv\) of multiplicity \(k \ge 3\), then removing one copy of \(uv\) and subdividing another gives \(G'\) with \(m\) edges and \(\tau (G') {\gt} \tau (G)\). So the extremizers of \(\mathrm{T}(m)\), \(m \ge 4\), have multiplicities \(\le 2\) (the bundle with \(n = 2\) has \(\tau = m\), while the triangle with multiplicities \(m-2, 1, 1\) has \(\tau = 2m - 3 {\gt} m\)). Among non-simple multigraphs the same holds for \(k \ge 4\).

Trust base: ⚠️ not checked. Written proof; not machine-checked. Control 5 of the paper: no counterexample on every multigraph with m ≤ 12, n ≤ 7

Proof: in this note·Covers: not formalized·Cite: Lemma 10, p. 5, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24561280

🆕 Classical and new: the paper: from (K2); no novelty claimed. The remark «what is not proved» explains why the step k = 3 fails in the non-simple class.

↳ Used in: Theorem 2, 3, 13

Proof ▼

With \(H = G\) minus one copy and \(R = R_{uv}(H)\), \(\tau (G) = \tau (H)(1 + R)\) and \(\tau (G') = \tau (H)(2 - R)\), so \(G'\) is better if and only if \(R {\lt} 1/2\). In \(H\) the \(k - 1 \ge 2\) copies of \(uv\) are in parallel with the rest, which has positive conductance between \(u\) and \(v\) when \(k = 3\) (else \(u\) or \(v\) is a cut vertex); hence \(R {\lt} 1/2\). For \(k \ge 4\), \(R \le 1/3\) and \(G'\) keeps a multiple edge.

Lemma 11 a multiple edge
#
📕 p. 5📍 map❝ cite⚠ report

If \(G\) has an edge \(uv\) of multiplicity \(k \ge 2\), then \((k-1)\, \tau (G) \le k\, \mathrm{T}(m-1)\), strictly if \(G\) is biconnected with \(n \ge 3\).

Trust base: ⚠️ not checked. Written proof; not machine-checked. Control 7 of the paper: the inequality holds for all 384 650 multiple edges of labelled connected multigraphs with n ≤ 6, m ≤ 8, strictly in the biconnected case (while τ ≤ T(m−1) fails 10 107 times)

Proof: in this note·Covers: not formalized·Cite: Lemma 11, p. 5, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24561280

🆕 Classical and new: the paper: from (K2) and (K3); no novelty claimed.

↳ Used in: Theorem 2, Corollary 12

Proof ▼

Let \(H\) be \(G\) minus one copy of \(uv\). In \(H\) the \(k - 1\) remaining copies join \(u\) and \(v\), so by (K3) \(R_{uv}(H) \le 1/(k-1)\), and by (K2) \(\tau (G) = \tau (H)(1 + R_{uv}(H)) \le \frac{k}{k-1}\mathrm{T}(m-1)\). If \(G\) is biconnected with \(n \ge 3\), the rest of \(H\) has positive conductance between \(u\) and \(v\) and the inequality for \(R\) is strict.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)
