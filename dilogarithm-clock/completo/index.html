The Clock That Never Ticks: a machine-checked path from the dilogarithm to quantum speed limits in Lean 4

🏷️ Special functions · the dilogarithm and the Clausen function

Carles Marín

At a glance

What the paper contributes

A sorry-free Lean 4 path from the dilogarithm, which Mathlib cites but does not define, to a quantum state that never orthogonalizes; version 2 adds its exact floor, in Lean and in Isabelle.

  • The real dilogarithm with Euler’s reflection, Landen’s transformation, the duplication formula and Li₂(1/2) (Theorem 1); the golden-ratio values and the Lee–Yang ceff = 2/5 from a 3×3 linear system, without the five-term relation.
  • The circle: the Clausen function and Catalan’s constant G = Cl₂(π/2); the Fejér–Jackson inequality (Theorem 2) and, by Abel summation without asymptotics, Cl₂(θ) ≥ sin(θ)/2 on (0, π).
  • Speed limits: Margolus–Levitin and an L¹ form of Mandelstam–Tamm for a scalar spectral measure (Theorem 3).
  • The assembly (Theorem 4): populations 6/(π²n²) on levels Eₙ = n have divergent first and second energy moments, so both bounds give nothing, and yet the state never reaches an orthogonal state. Version 2 gives the one-line reason (the lowest level carries more than half of the weight) and shows that the Zieliński–Zych limits see exactly that (Proposition 1).
  • The exact floor (Theorem 5, Corollary 1): |S| ≥ 1 − 21−s for every zeta state pₙ ∝ n−s, also where no level dominates.
  • The mathematics is classical; the paper claims the formalization, the assembly and the place of the zeta state among the speed limits.

Abstract.

Mathlib’s source contains a comment referring to the dilogarithm \({\mathrm{Li}_2}\) — a function the library does not have. We fill that hole and follow the questions where they lead. The result is a sorry-free Lean 4 development containing the real dilogarithm with Euler’s reflection identity, Landen’s transformation, the duplication formula, and the golden-ratio ladder \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(1/\varphi ^2)=\pi ^2/15-\ln ^2\varphi \), \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(1/\varphi )=\pi ^2/10-\ln ^2\varphi \) (derived from a \(3\times 3\) linear system — no five-term relation needed); the Rogers value \(\operatorname{L}(1/\varphi ^2)=\pi ^2/15\), equivalently the Lee–Yang effective central charge \(c_{\mathrm{eff}}=2/5\); the Clausen function \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\) and Catalan’s constant \(G={\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\pi /2)\); the Fejér–Jackson inequality \(\sum _{k=1}^{M}\sin (k\theta )/k{\gt}0\) on \((0,\pi )\); the positivity \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )\ge \sin (\theta )/2\) by an asymptotics-free Abel summation; and the Margolus–Levitin and (an \(L^1\) form of the) Mandelstam–Tamm quantum speed limits. When version 1 appeared (June 2026) none of these was in Lean; Isabelle’s Archive of Formal Proofs already had the polylogarithm of integer order with its duplication formula (Eberl, 2023), which our search had missed. The pieces assemble into the ending the title promises: the quantum state with populations \(\propto 1/n^2\) on equally spaced levels has divergent first and second energy moments — both textbook speed limits give no bound — and yet never reaches a state orthogonal to itself (a “tick”, in this paper, is exactly that). Version 2 is exact about why. Its lowest level carries \(6/\pi ^2{\gt}1/2\) of the population, which alone forbids orthogonality (Uffink’s cut-off inequality), and the fractional-moment speed limits of Zieliński and Zych reduce, as their order tends to zero, to exactly this criterion. The dilogarithm says more: the modulus of the autocorrelation never drops below \(1/2\), its value at half period. For the whole family \(p_n\propto n^{-s}\), \(s{\gt}1\), we formalize the exact floor \(\inf _\theta |S(\theta )|=1-2^{1-s}\), from \(|\mathrm{Li}_s(z)|\ge \eta (s)\) on the unit circle for every real \(s{\gt}0\); it holds also for \(1{\lt}s{\lt}s^\ast \approx 1.7286\), where no level dominates and none of the Margolus–Levitin, Mandelstam–Tamm or Zieliński–Zych bounds certifies that orthogonality never occurs. We also formalize the Zieliński–Zych bound for orders up to \(1\), including the inequality behind it, and exact examples showing that log-convex or three times monotone populations need not have their smallest autocorrelation modulus at half period. We are exact about the boundary: every identity and inequality here is classical; the contribution is the machine-checked development, its assembly, and the place of the zeta-state theorem among the speed limits. Every named theorem depends only on the three standard axioms.

1 The question that starts it: why does the library cite a function it does not have?

Few functions have been discovered as many times as the dilogarithm. Euler wrote down its series in the 1760s; within a century it had been met again by Spence, by Abel in the study of its functional equations, by Lobachevsky in the measurement of hyperbolic space, and by Kummer — each giving it a different name and a different reason to care. Two centuries later it reappeared, unbidden, in the finite parts of Feynman integrals and in the thermodynamics of integrable systems. It is the rare non-elementary function that now and then consents to a closed form, and the catalogue of those closed forms reads like a tour of mathematics. So there is something almost comic in the fact that, in 2026 — after a decade of one of the most ambitious formalization efforts ever attempted — the dilogarithm was not in the library at all. The machines we now use to certify theorems had never been told what \({\mathrm{Li}_2}\) is. They had, however, been told to mention it.

The file Mathlib/Analysis/SpecialFunctions/Integrals/LogTrigonometric.lean of Mathlib — the mathematical library of the Lean 4 proof assistant, on which a substantial part of contemporary formalized mathematics is built — describes one of its integrals by referring to the dilogarithm

\[ {\mathrm{Li}_2}(x) \; =\; \sum _{n\ge 1}\frac{x^n}{n^2}. \]

The function itself is absent. When version 1 of this paper appeared (June 2026) it was absent from Mathlib, and the other Lean developments of it that our October search found date from September 2026 on. It was not absent from every proof assistant, as version 1 said: Isabelle’s Archive of Formal Proofs has had, since November 2023, Eberl’s polylogarithm \(\mathrm{Li}_s\) of integer order, with its derivative, \(\mathrm{Li}_1(z)=-\ln (1-z)\) and the duplication formula  [ 24 ] . The reflection and Landen identities, the golden values, the Rogers function, the Clausen function, Catalan’s constant, the Fejér–Jackson inequality and the quantum speed limits are not there, and we found none of them in the other proof assistants we searched; the existing quantum libraries (CoqQ, SQIR, Isabelle Marries Dirac) formalize circuits, not dynamical bounds. 1

How does a function this central stay unformalized for a decade of Mathlib? Because it sits in an odd blind spot: too special to be infrastructure, too analytic to be algebra, and guarded — as we will see — by exactly one genuinely delicate limit. The genesis of this project was an audit: a fifty-digit numerical verification of the dilogarithm identities in a recent preprint of Alha  [ 23 ] . Auditing someone else’s dilogarithms sent us to the library for a reference implementation; the library sent us back empty-handed. We decided to fill the hole, and to keep asking the next question until the questions stopped. They stopped five sections later, inside a quantum mechanics problem — and, in version 2, one section after that. This paper is the travelogue.

2 Q1: what does the series know about its own derivative?

History bead. The function is Euler’s (1768); the reflection identity below is his. Landen’s transformation is from 1780. Everything in this section is classical (Lewin  [ 1 ] , Zagier  [ 2 ] ); the Lean is Dilog/Basic.lean.

A power series differentiates termwise, and the dilogarithm’s derivative collapses to elementary functions:

\[ {\mathrm{Li}_2}'(x) \; =\; \sum _{n\ge 1}\frac{x^{n-1}}{n} \; =\; -\frac{\ln (1-x)}{x} \qquad (\lvert x\rvert {\lt}1,\ x\ne 0). \]

In Lean this single line is the analytic core of the whole development — term-by-term differentiation under a geometric majorant (hasDerivAt_tsum_of_isPreconnected), then the Mercator series to identify the sum. Once the derivative is known, functional equations become mechanical:

Recipe: a functional equation in three moves To prove a combination \(F\) of dilogarithms and logarithms is constant on an interval: (1) differentiate termwise and check \(F'=0\) by chain/product rules plus field_simp; ring; (2) conclude \(F\) is constant by mean-value machinery (constant_of_has_deriv_right_zero on subintervals); (3) evaluate the constant as a boundary limit. Step (3) is where the one delicate limit of the subject lives: \(\ln x\cdot \ln (1-x)\to 0\) at the endpoint, tamed as \(\big(\ln (1-x)/x\big)\cdot \big(x\ln x\big)\to (-1)\cdot 0\) via the slope characterization of the derivative and tendsto_log_mul_rpow_nhdsGT_zero. Continuity of \({\mathrm{Li}_2}\) up to the boundary is the Weierstrass \(M\)-test (continuousOn_tsum).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

The dilogarithm is the series \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(x)=\sum _{n\ge 1}x^n/n^2\). Its value at a point says little by itself, but its values at related points are tied by exact identities: the value at \(x\) and at \(1-x\) (Euler’s reflection), at \(x\) and at \(x/(x-1)\) (Landen), at \(x\), \(-x\) and \(x^2\) (duplication). Each identity is proved the same way: differentiate the combination, see that the derivative is zero, so the combination is constant, and find the constant as a limit at the edge of the interval. The duplication needs no calculus: it is the series split into even and odd terms. Put \(x=\tfrac 12\) in the reflection and the two sides coincide, which gives \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(\tfrac 12)\). Section 3 of the paper then uses the three identities at the golden-ratio points as three linear equations, which give the golden values without Abel’s five-term relation. The domains are the ones of the series: Landen stops at \(x=\tfrac 12\) because past it \(x/(x-1)\) leaves the disc where the series converges.

Two honesty notes, both visible in the Lean statements. The Landen domain stops at \(x=\frac12\): past it, the argument \(x/(x-1)\) leaves the disc of convergence, and a power-series definition does not analytically continue — we do not pretend it does. And the duplication formula needs no calculus at all: it is a pure rearrangement of the series by even and odd indices (tsum_even_add_odd), the formal mirror of how Euler would have proved it. (Isabelle’s AFP proves it too, for every integer order  [ 24 ] .)

From theory to practice. The dilogarithm is the standard weight-2 function of one-loop Feynman integrals and of hyperbolic and \(K\)-theoretic invariants; textbooks reach for it constantly. The practical value of formalizing it is modest and specific: a verified Li2 with its functional equations — including the one delicate boundary limit — that downstream Lean developments can import rather than re-derive.

The turn. Three functional equations in hand, the classical showpieces of the subject are the golden-ratio evaluations — and one route to them goes through Abel’s five-term relation, a two-variable identity this paper does not formalize (its sequel does  [ 27 ] ). Are we stuck?

3 Q2: can the golden ladder be climbed without Abel’s relation?

History bead. The values are Landen’s (1780); the two-term sum is from Rogers’ 1907 memoir  [ 4 ] . Their role as a central charge belongs to the thermodynamic Bethe ansatz of the 1990s  [ 15 , 16 , 17 ] .

The answer is yes, and the key is two arithmetic accidents of the golden ratio \(\varphi =(1+\sqrt5)/2\), both one-liners from \(\varphi ^2=\varphi +1\):

\[ \frac{1}{\varphi }+\frac{1}{\varphi ^2}=1 \qquad \text{and}\qquad \frac{1/\varphi ^2}{1/\varphi ^2-1}=-\frac{1}{\varphi }. \]

The first says \(1/\varphi \) and \(1/\varphi ^2\) are a reflection pair; the second says Landen maps \(1/\varphi ^2\) to \(-1/\varphi \) — and note \(1/\varphi ^2\approx 0.382{\lt}\tfrac 12\), inside our proven Landen domain. Reflection at \(1/\varphi ^2\), Landen at \(1/\varphi ^2\), duplication at \(1/\varphi \): three linear equations, three unknowns, and linarith closes the system.

\(\displaystyle {\mathrm{Li}_2}\! \Big(\tfrac {1}{\varphi ^2}\Big)=\tfrac {\pi ^2}{15}-\ln ^2\varphi , \qquad {\mathrm{Li}_2}\! \Big(\tfrac {1}{\varphi }\Big)=\tfrac {\pi ^2}{10}-\ln ^2\varphi , \qquad {\mathrm{Li}_2}\! \Big(-\tfrac {1}{\varphi }\Big)=\tfrac {\ln ^2\varphi }{2}-\tfrac {\pi ^2}{15}. \)

No five-term relation is needed — not a new route (the values are Landen’s, older than Abel’s relation), but one the formalization makes explicit. In the Rogers normalization \(\operatorname{L}(x)={\mathrm{Li}_2}(x)+\tfrac 12\ln x\ln (1-x)\) the \(\ln ^2\) defects cancel (rogersL_inv_goldenRatio_sq, rogersL_gold_sum):

\[ \operatorname{L}\! \Big(\tfrac {1}{\varphi ^2}\Big)=\frac{\pi ^2}{15},\qquad \operatorname{L}\! \Big(\tfrac {1}{\varphi }\Big)+\operatorname{L}\! \Big(\tfrac {1}{\varphi ^2}\Big) =\frac{\pi ^2}{6}=\operatorname{L}(1), \]

and the first, normalized, is the effective central charge of the Lee–Yang thermodynamic Bethe ansatz, \(c_{\mathrm{eff}}=\operatorname{L}(1/\varphi ^2)/\operatorname{L}(1)=2/5\) — to our knowledge the first TBA dilogarithm identity verified in a proof assistant.

From theory to practice. The value \(c_{\mathrm{eff}}=2/5\) is the effective central charge of the Lee–Yang model, a non-unitary minimal CFT whose Yang–Lee zeros have been observed experimentally in spin systems. The identity formalized here is the simplest entry of the thermodynamic-Bethe-ansatz dilogarithm table; having one machine-checked anchors any later formalization of integrable-model thermodynamics.

The turn. The ladder spends the function’s life inside the interval of convergence. The natural next territory is the boundary itself. What does the series do on the unit circle?

4 Q3: what happens on the circle?

History bead. Clausen introduced his function in 1832  [ 3 ] ; Catalan’s constant is from his 1865 memoir, and its irrationality is, famously, still open. The cosine series below is the Fourier expansion of the Bernoulli polynomial \(B_2\), which Mathlib already owns (hasSum_one_div_nat_pow_mul_cos); the Lean of this section is Dilog/Clausen.lean.

On the circle the dilogarithm splits into a tame half and a wild half:

\[ {\mathrm{Li}_2}(e^{i\theta }) \; =\; \underbrace{\sum _{n\ge 1}\frac{\cos n\theta }{n^2}}_{\textstyle =\; \frac{\pi ^2}{6}-\frac{\pi \theta }{2}+\frac{\theta ^2}{4}} \; +\; i\, \underbrace{\sum _{n\ge 1}\frac{\sin n\theta }{n^2}}_{\textstyle =\; {\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )} \qquad (\theta \in [0,2\pi ]). \]

The real part is an elementary parabola (hasSum_cos_div_sq, from Mathlib’s Bernoulli–Fourier machinery at \(k=1\)). The imaginary part is not elementary at all: it is the Clausen function, a genuinely new special function — the reason this section exists. At the quarter circle it produces a celebrity:

\[ G \; =\; {\mathrm{Cl}_2}\! \Big(\frac{\pi }{2}\Big) \; =\; \sum _{k\ge 0}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)^2} \; =\; 0.9159\ldots , \]

Catalan’s constant, absent from every proof assistant we searched when version 1 appeared (it has since been defined independently in LeanPolyLog  [ 26 ] ) (catalanConst, Cl₂_pi_div_two; the proof is the same even/odd rearrangement as the duplication formula — even-indexed sines vanish, odd-indexed give the alternating series). Basic symmetries come for free: \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}(0)={\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\pi )=0\), \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\) is odd, and \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}(2\pi -\theta )=-{\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )\).

From theory to practice. \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\) is, up to scale, Lobachevsky’s function: the volume of an ideal hyperbolic tetrahedron is a sum of three \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\) values, so this is the analytic core of hyperbolic \(3\)-manifold and knot-complement volumes. Catalan’s constant appears as the dimer entropy \(G/\pi \) of the square lattice. A verified \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\) is a concrete step toward machine-checked hyperbolic volumes and lattice-entropy constants.

The turn. Plot the wild half (Figure 2) and one suspicion dominates everything: between \(0\) and \(\pi \) the function looks positive. Two values and a hunch are not a theorem. Why is it positive? That question is older than it looks: it is Fejér’s, from 1910.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Add the waves \(\sin \theta \), \(\tfrac 12\sin 2\theta \), \(\tfrac 13\sin 3\theta \), …, stopping anywhere: between \(0\) and \(\pi \) the total is always positive. For two terms one can see it by hand, \(\sin \theta +\tfrac 12\sin 2\theta =\sin \theta \, (1+\cos \theta )\). For any number of terms the proof looks at the lowest point of the sum inside \((0,\pi )\): there the derivative vanishes, that equation factors, and either way the last wave is not negative at that point, so the minimum is at least the previous sum, positive by induction. The paper then uses these positive sums to bound the Clausen function \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )=\sum \sin (n\theta )/n^2\): it is at least \(\sin (\theta )/2\) on \((0,\pi )\), by a summation by parts whose every term is positive, with no estimate of the tail. At \(\theta =\pi /2\) the Clausen function is Catalan’s constant \(G\approx 0.9160\).

The partial sums \(S_M(\theta )=\sum _{k\le M}\sin (k\theta )/k\) as a surface over \((\theta , M)\): positive on \((0,\pi )\) for every \(M\) (Theorem 2). Below, \(\mathrm{Cl}_2\) with the bound \(\sin (\theta )/2\) that the Abel summation gives, and Catalan’s constant at \(\pi /2\).

Hand-checkable case. \(M=2\): \(\sin \theta +\tfrac 12\sin 2\theta =\sin \theta \, (1+\cos \theta ){\gt}0\) on \((0,\pi )\). The point of the formalization is that the same certainty now covers every \(M\).

The proof is the classical interior-minimum induction, with two touches worth recording. First, the Dirichlet kernel is handled multiplicatively — no division, no removable singularity:

\[ 2\sin \tfrac {\theta }{2}\sum _{k=1}^{M}\cos k\theta =\sin \! \big((M+\tfrac 12)\theta \big)-\sin \tfrac {\theta }{2}, \]

a telescope of \(\sin ((k+\tfrac 12)\theta )\) via the product-to-sum identity (Finset.sum_range_sub). Second, at an interior local minimum the critical equation \(\sin ((M+\tfrac 12)\theta _0)=\sin (\theta _0/2)\) factors (Real.sin_sub_sin) as \(\sin (M\theta _0/2)\cos ((M{+}1)\theta _0/2)=0\), and its two branches force

\[ \sin (M\theta _0)=0 \qquad \text{or}\qquad \sin (M\theta _0)=\sin \theta _0{\gt}0: \]

either way the last term of the sum is nonnegative at the minimum, so the minimum value is at least the previous partial sum, \(S_M(\theta _0)\ge S_{M-1}(\theta _0){\gt}0\) by induction. Boundary values vanish; compactness closes.

From Fejér–Jackson to the Clausen function is an Abel summation, and here the formalization found a small simplification: no asymptotics are needed. Writing the \((N{+}1)\)-term partial sum of the \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\) series in terms of the Fejér–Jackson sums \(S_M\),

\[ \sum _{n=1}^{N+1}\frac{\sin n\theta }{n^2} =\frac{S_{N+1}(\theta )}{N+1} +\sum _{k=1}^{N}S_k(\theta )\Big(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}\Big), \]

every piece on the right is positive, and the \(k=1\) summand alone equals \(\sin (\theta )/2\). Every partial sum is therefore \(\ge \sin (\theta )/2\) — no harmonic-number bounds, no tail estimates — and the limit inherits the bound (Cl₂_pos; as a theorem of its own, sin_div_two_le_Cl₂ in  [ 28 ] ):

\(\displaystyle {\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )\; \ge \; \frac{\sin \theta }{2}\; {\gt}\; 0\qquad (0{\lt}\theta {\lt}\pi ).\)

The turn — the big one, and the honest one. Step back and look at what we are holding: a sum \(\sum p_n e^{-in\theta }\) with positive weights \(p_n\propto 1/n^2\), whose imaginary part never vanishes on \((0,\pi )\). A physicist reads that expression instantly — it is the autocorrelation of a quantum state, the inner product between a state and its own time evolution, for populations \(p_n\) on equally spaced energy levels. We must be honest about how this reading entered the project, because it did not grow out of the chain of questions: a parallel thread — born from asking which part of the old “universe as a computer” metaphor is an actual theorem — had already formalized the two classical bounds on how fast any quantum state can evolve. At this point the two threads crossed: the circle had been measuring time all along, and the positivity theorem we had just proved was a statement about a quantum system that cannot reach an orthogonal state. History bead. Mandelstam and Tamm bounded the orthogonalization time by the energy spread in 1945  [ 8 ] ; Margolus and Levitin bounded it by the mean energy in 1998  [ 9 ] . Together they are the textbook “quantum speed limits”: how many distinguishable states can a system visit per unit time, on a given energy budget. The Lean is QSL/, and, for what version 2 adds, Physics/QuantumMechanics/SpeedLimit/ in the formalizations repository  [ 28 ] .

A state \(\lvert \psi _0\rangle =\sum _n c_n\lvert E_n\rangle \) evolves with autocorrelation \(S(t)=\sum _n p_n e^{-iE_nt}\), \(p_n=\lvert c_n\rvert ^2\), and reaches an orthogonal state at time \(\tau \) iff \(S(\tau )=0\). This is a statement about a scalar function of a discrete nonnegative spectral measure, and that is how we formalize it — the Hilbert space is bookkeeping. 💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

A quantum state that has evolved for a time \(\tau \) is fully distinguishable from where it started exactly when its autocorrelation \(S(\tau )=\sum _np_ne^{-iE_n\tau }\) is zero. The two speed limits say it cannot happen too soon. Margolus and Levitin, with energies measured from a ground level \(E_n\ge 0\): the time is at least \(\pi /(2\langle E\rangle )\), so a small mean energy forces a slow state. The second bound uses the mean absolute deviation of the energy \(D_1\): the time is at least \(1/D_1\); since \(D_1\le \Delta E\), it gives the textbook weak Mandelstam–Tamm bound \(\tau \ge 1/\Delta E\) (the sharp constant \(\pi /2\) is left open). Both are stated for the autocorrelation of a list of weights and energies, with no Hilbert space. The key step of the first is the inequality \(\cos x\ge 1-\tfrac 2\pi (x+\sin x)\) for \(x\ge 0\). Both bounds say nothing when the mean energy or the spread is infinite, which is the case of Theorem 4.

The heart of Margolus–Levitin is one inequality of independent interest, \(\cos x\ge 1-\tfrac {2}{\pi }(x+\sin x)\) for \(x\ge 0\) (equality at \(0\) and \(\pi \)), which we prove by a rise-then-fall argument: both endpoints zero, split at \(x_0=2\arctan (2/\pi )\). The same shape — both endpoints at zero, and the critical-point equation analysed at an interior extremum — carries the Fejér–Jackson proof above; that is a transferable technique. Since \(D_1\le \Delta E\) by Cauchy–Schwarz, the second bound implies the textbook weak Mandelstam–Tamm \(\tau \ge 1/\Delta E\) (mandelstam_tamm_weak); the sharp constant \(\pi /2\) needs the geodesic argument and is left as a road (§9).

\[ S(\theta )=\frac{6}{\pi ^2}\, {\mathrm{Li}_2}(e^{-i\theta }) =\frac{6}{\pi ^2}\Big( \underbrace{\tfrac {\pi ^2}{6}-\tfrac {\pi \theta }{2}+\tfrac {\theta ^2}{4}}_{\text{the parabola of \S 4}} \; -\; i\, \underbrace{{\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )}_{\text{positive, by \S 5}}\Big). \]

From theory to practice. Quantum speed limits set the minimum time for a gate, a measurement, or a state transfer on a given energy budget; they recur across quantum computing, metrology, and quantum thermodynamics (battery charging, optimal control). The zeta state carries a practical lesson: a large energy budget does not buy an orthogonal state if more than half of the weight stays on one level — reaching an orthogonal state needs the weight spread over levels, not merely energy in quantity.

7 Q6: what decides orthogonality when no level dominates?

Take the whole family of zeta states, \(p_n=n^{-s}/\zeta (s)\) on the levels \(E_n=n\), \(s{\gt}1\). The first energy moment diverges for \(s\le 2\) and the second for \(s\le 3\). The lowest level carries \(p_1=1/\zeta (s)\), and \(p_1{\lt}\tfrac 12\) exactly when \(\zeta (s){\gt}2\), that is for \(1{\lt}s{\lt}s^\ast \) with \(s^\ast =1.72864723899818\ldots \) (\(\zeta (s^\ast )=2\)). In that range the triangle inequality says nothing, Margolus–Levitin and Mandelstam–Tamm say nothing, and every Zieliński–Zych bound is finite, as is their supremum over \(\alpha \) (with \(E_0=1\), the lowest level: numerically \(0.0016\) at \(s=1.3\), \(0.066\) at \(s=1.5\), \(0.45\) at \(s=1.7\); the bound tends to \(0\) at both ends of the range of \(\alpha \), by Proposition 1 and because \(M_\alpha \to \infty \) as \(\alpha \to s-1\)). These bounds certify a positive time before which the state cannot be orthogonal; none of them decides whether it ever is.

Uffink’s cut-off with more than one level does decide it, one state at a time: keeping the first \(N\) levels, \(\lvert S\rvert \ge \min _\theta \big\lvert \sum _{n\le N}p_ne^{-in\theta }\big\rvert -\sum _{n{\gt}N}p_n\), and the right-hand side is positive for \(N=3\) at \(s=1.7\), \(N=8\) at \(s=1.5\) and \(N=200\) at \(s=1.3\) (numerically: \(0.091\), \(0.023\), \(0.015\)). It needs a certified minimum of a trigonometric polynomial for each \(s\), a number of levels that grows as \(s\to 1\), and it gives a floor far below the true one (\(0.384\), \(0.293\), \(0.188\)). Two arguments settle the whole family at once, the second with the exact floor.

The first is the shape of the populations: they decrease strictly, and a convergent power series with strictly decreasing positive coefficients has no zero on the unit circle. For polynomials this, and the description of the zeros on the circle when the decrease is not strict (they are roots of unity, and the coefficients come in groups of equal consecutive values), is Hurwitz’s 1913 complement  [ 19 , § 2 ] to the Eneström–Kakeya theorem  [ 18 ] . Version 1 stated it as Remark 1 and wrote that it had not found it in the literature; it is Hurwitz’s. In Lean, for summable non-increasing series, it is enestrom_kakeya_boundary_series and strictAnti_series_ne_zero. Physically: a state with strictly decreasing populations on equally spaced levels never becomes orthogonal; a state with non-increasing populations that does orthogonalize has its decrements on an arithmetic progression, like the Margolus–Levitin-sharp state \((\lvert 0\rangle +\lvert E\rangle )/\sqrt2\).

The second gives the exact floor.

The coefficients are moments: \((k+1)^{-s}=\int _0^1t^k\, d\mu _s(t)\) with the positive measure \(d\mu _s=(-\ln t)^{s-1}dt/\Gamma (s)\), so that \(\mathrm{Li}_s(z)/z=\int _0^1d\mu _s(t)/(1-tz)\) inside the disc — the representation of \(\mathrm{Li}_s(z)/z\) as a Stieltjes transform of Roth, Ruscheweyh and Salinas  [ 21 , p. 3 ] . \[ \operatorname {Re}\frac{1}{1-tz}-\frac{1}{1+t} =\frac{t\, (1+\cos \theta )(1-t)}{(1+t)\, \lvert 1-tz\rvert ^{2}}\; \ge \; 0 . \]

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Give the levels \(n=1,2,3,\dots \) weights proportional to \(n^{-s}\), for any \(s{\gt}1\). Then the overlap of the state with its starting point never drops below \(1-2^{1-s}\), and it reaches that value at half period. For \(s=2\) the floor is \(\tfrac 12\). The point is the range \(1{\lt}s{\lt}1.7286\ldots \): there no level carries more than half of the weight, the speed limits built from energy moments certify nothing, and yet the floor holds.

The theorem is classical: it is a consequence of Fejér’s theorem on four times monotone coefficients, which make \(\operatorname {Re}f(e^{i\theta })\) decrease on \([0,\pi ]\) (partial sums of the transform, [ 7 , p. 104 ] ) and \(\lvert f(e^{i\theta })\rvert ^2\) decrease on \([0,\pi ]\) [ 7 , p. 107 ] ; for \(s=2\) it is Boyer and Parry’s lemma  [ 20 ] . The proof above is the one we formalized; its only estimate is the displayed identity. The same proof gives the floor for every population that is a mixture of geometric populations: \(p_n=\int t^n\, d\mu (t)\), \(n\ge 0\), with \(\mu \) a positive measure on \([0,1)\) normalized by \(\sum _np_n=\int d\mu /(1-t)=1\) (absence of mass at \(t=1\) alone does not make the populations summable: the uniform \(\mu \) gives \(p_n=1/(n+1)\)) (le_norm_of_tendsto; for finite mixtures antipode_floor, already in June). Physically these are the populations of mixtures of thermal states of a harmonic oscillator, read as the populations of a pure state on the levels \(0,1,2,\dots \): every such state never becomes orthogonal, and the modulus of its autocorrelation is smallest at half period, where it equals \(\sum _n(-1)^np_n\). (A shift of the energy origin, as from levels \(n\ge 1\) to \(n\ge 0\), multiplies \(S\) by a phase: the modulus, which is what orthogonality sees, does not change; the real part and the sign at \(\theta =\pi \) do.) We have not found this reading in the physics literature; the closest work on the zeros of survival amplitudes argues from the dominant terms and leaves out equally spaced spectra  [ 14 ] .

8 The boundary of the contribution

Everything here that is mathematics is classical: Euler (1768), Landen (1780), Clausen (1832), Rogers (1907), Fejér (1910, 1937), Jackson (1911), Hurwitz (1913), Mandelstam–Tamm (1945), Margolus–Levitin (1998), Uffink (1993); the TBA reading of the golden values is from the 1990s; the floor of \(\lvert {\mathrm{Li}_2}\rvert \) on the circle follows from Fejér (1937) and is stated by Boyer and Parry (2021); the fractional-moment bounds are Zieliński and Zych’s (2006). We claim no new identity and no new inequality of substance. The contributions are: (i) the machine-checked development itself — in June 2026 the first formalization, as far as we could determine, of each item of the abstract except the polylogarithm and its duplication formula, which Isabelle’s AFP had  [ 24 ] ; (ii) three proof-engineering choices that may be reusable: the division-free Dirichlet telescope, the asymptotics-free Abel bound \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\ge \sin (\theta )/2\), and the statements of the speed limits as facts about a scalar spectral measure (fully real for Margolus–Levitin; Mandelstam–Tamm uses the complex autocorrelation); (iii) the assembly of Theorem 4, and, in version 2, its honest place: it follows from one dominant level, which the moment-based speed limits see only in the limit of Proposition 1; (iv) the floor of every normalizable zeta state, \(s{\gt}1\) (Corollary 1), as a statement about speed limits where the moment-based bounds are silent, from the analytic floor of Theorem 5, formalized for every real \(s{\gt}0\), with the proof of the Zieliński–Zych inequality for \(0{\lt}\alpha \le 1\) and the exact examples of the last table. The physical readings in (iii) and (iv) are ones we have not seen stated; we would not be surprised to find them folklore among polylogarithm or speed-limit specialists.

Related formalization. We build directly on Mathlib’s analytic infrastructure: term-by-term differentiation, the Weierstrass \(M\)-test, mean-value machinery, and the Bernoulli–Fourier expansion that hands us the parabola for free. Special functions in the proof-assistant literature have centred on \(\Gamma \), \(\zeta \) and the elementary transcendental functions; Isabelle has the polylogarithm of integer order  [ 24 ] , and since version 1 two Lean 4 developments have formalized parts of the real dilogarithm  [ 25 , 26 ] . On the quantum side, the established libraries (CoqQ, SQIR, Isabelle Marries Dirac) formalize circuits and gate semantics rather than dynamical bounds on continuous evolution. This development sits in the gap between those bodies of work.

On methodology: the formalization was carried out in collaboration with an AI assistant (Claude, Anthropic; version 2 with Claude Opus), with every step gated by the Lean kernel, every identity checked independently at 20–50 digits before formalization, and every named theorem verified by #print axioms to depend only on propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound. Correctness does not rest on the assistant: the Lean kernel checks every proof, so the AI affected the path to each theorem, not its validity. The literature checks of version 2 were also done with AI assistance; every source cited for a statement was read in the original, except Uffink’s note, which we cite through Andrews. The mathematics, the mistakes, and the claims remain the author’s responsibility.

9 Open roads

  1. After the five-term relation. Abel’s five-term relation, the capstone of weight 2, was formalized in the sequel to version 1  [ 27 ] ; with it, every two-variable dilogarithm identity is within reach of the same machinery.

  2. The complex dilogarithm and the Bloch–Wigner function; with them, the 2D dimer entropy (\(G/\pi \) — Catalan again) and hyperbolic volumes come into reach. (The boundary values \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(e^{i\theta })\) and \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(i)=-\pi ^2/48+iG\) are now in Lean  [ 28 ] .)

  3. Sharp Mandelstam–Tamm (\(\pi /2\Delta E\), the geodesic argument) and the arbitrary-fidelity Margolus–Levitin bound — conjectured in 2003, proven only in 2023  [ 22 ] : a fresh theorem to formalize.

  4. A speed limit that sees the shape of the spectral measure. The exact class of populations whose autocorrelation modulus is smallest at half period is not known to us, and it is not convex: \((10,4,1)/15\) and \((1,4,10)/15\) both have their smallest modulus only at \(\theta =\pi \), but their average \((11,8,11)/30\) vanishes where \(\cos \theta =-4/11\). The auxiliary class defined by the real part, \(\operatorname {Re}F_p(e^{i\theta })\ge F_p(-1)\), is linear in the populations, hence a convex cone for finitely many levels, which a Fejér–Riesz factorization should describe; for the modulus, Fejér–Riesz applies to the trigonometric polynomial \(\lvert F_p(e^{i\theta })\rvert ^2-F_p(-1)^2\), which is quadratic in \(p\).

  5. Fejér’s four times monotone theorem  [ 7 ] in Lean, and the positive trigonometric sums beyond Fejér–Jackson (Vietoris) — the toolkit is in place.

Questions to take with you

A paper built from questions should end with the ones it could not answer, one per station of the map. Two of them (on Q1 and Q3) are pedagogical rather than research questions, and we say so.

  1. Weight 1 (logarithms) needs no delicate limits; weight 2 needed exactly one (\(\ln x\cdot \ln (1-x)\to 0\), the only place our formalization had to fight). Is the number of genuinely delicate boundary limits an invariant of the weight — and what does it count? (To be a mathematical question it needs a notion of “delicate” independent of the choices of a formalization.)

  2. We reached Landen’s golden values by replacing one two-variable identity (Abel’s) with three one-variable ones, because the golden point sits where reflection and Landen orbits intersect. Which specializations of the five-term relation are reachable this way — precisely: what is the subgroup of relations generated by reflection, Landen and duplication inside the full functional-equation group of \({\mathrm{Li}_2}\), and is the five-term relation in it?

  3. On the circle, the even-weight cosine series is a polynomial and the sine series is transcendentally new; at odd weight the roles swap. What does weight parity know about the boundary that makes it exchange the tame and the wild half? (The classical answer is the Fourier series of the Bernoulli polynomials; the question is how to make a formal library use it uniformly in the weight.)

  4. Two positivity proofs in this development end the same way: both endpoints at zero and the critical-point equation at an interior extremum (Fejér–Jackson and the Margolus–Levitin cosine inequality). The positivity of \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}\) is inherited from Fejér–Jackson by Abel summation here, although classically it also follows from \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}'(\theta )=-\ln (2\sin (\theta /2))\) by the same rise-then-fall shape. Is there a single theorem behind them — a maximum principle for positive sums — and would it be the right thing to formalize next?

  5. The zeta state defeats both textbook speed limits through its infinite moments, and the fractional-moment limits see it only through its lowest level. Exact moments can sometimes pin the whole distribution: for \(E\ge 0\) with \(\langle E\rangle =\langle E^2\rangle =\langle E^3\rangle =\frac45\) one has \(\langle E(E-1)^2\rangle =0\), so the populations are \((\frac15,\frac45)\) on \(\{ 0,1\} \) and \(\lvert S\rvert \ge \frac35\), with no dominant lowest level. What can be certified from finitely many moments known only approximately, or for spectral measures with infinite support, where no finite set of moments pins the support? (Zieliński and Zych’s doublet example  [ 11 ] shows how far the moments can move without changing the orthogonalization time.)

  6. Complete monotonicity and four times monotonicity put the smallest modulus at half period; three times monotonicity and log-convexity (hence convexity) do not. What is the exact class — not a convex one (Section 9) — and is it, as for Hurwitz’s theorem, a condition one can read off the differences of the populations?

Techniques (10)

A functional equation in three moves

To prove that a combination $F$ of dilogarithms and logarithms is constant: (1) differentiate term by term and check $F^\prime=0$; (2) conclude $F$ is constant by the mean value theorem on subintervals; (3) find the constant as a limit at the edge of the interval.
🔁 also used in: The Pentagon, Machine-Checked (the five-term relation, same argument in two variables)
↳ Theorem 1
📄 from the paper §2 (Recipe box)

Replace a two-variable identity by one-variable ones at an orbit intersection

The golden values come from reflection at $1/\varphi^2$, Landen at $1/\varphi^2$ and duplication at $1/\varphi$: three linear equations in three unknowns, closed by linarith. Not a new route (the values are Landen’s, older than Abel’s relation), but one the formalization makes explicit.
↳ Theorem 1
📄 from the paper §3 and «Questions to take with you», Q2

Split a series into even and odd terms

The duplication formula $\mathrm{Li}_2(x^2)=2(\mathrm{Li}_2(x)+\mathrm{Li}_2(-x))$, Catalan's constant $G=\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/2)$ and $\eta(s)=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$ are all a rearrangement by parity (tsum_even_add_odd), with no calculus.
↳ Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Corollary 1
📄 from the paper §2, §4 and §7

Both ends at zero, the critical equation at an interior extremum

At an interior minimum of the partial sum the critical equation factors, and either branch makes the last term nonnegative there; so the minimum is at least the previous sum, positive by induction. The same shape proves two positivity statements of the development: Fejér–Jackson and the cosine inequality of Margolus–Levitin; $\mathrm{Cl}_2>0$ is inherited from Fejér–Jackson by Abel summation.
↳ Theorem 2, Theorem 3

A telescope without division

The Dirichlet kernel handled multiplicatively, $2\sin\tfrac\theta2\sum_{k\le M}\cos k\theta=\sin((M+\tfrac12)\theta)-\sin\tfrac\theta2$: no quotient, no removable singularity to treat in Lean.

Abel summation that keeps its first term as the bound

Writing the partial sums of $\mathrm{Cl}_2$ through the Fejér–Jackson sums, every term is positive and the $k=1$ term alone is $\sin(\theta)/2$: the bound $\mathrm{Cl}_2(\theta)\ge\sin(\theta)/2$ with no tail estimate and no asymptotics.

State the physics as a statement about numbers

The speed limits are stated for the autocorrelation of a discrete spectral measure (weights $p_n\ge0$, energies $E_n$); the Hilbert space is bookkeeping, and Margolus–Levitin needs no complex numbers.

One level against all the others (the triangle inequality)

If one population exceeds $\tfrac12$, the others cannot cancel it: $|S|\ge2p_j-1$ at every time. It needs no moment of the energy, so it works where the textbook limits fail.

Read a zero on the circle from the coefficients

Multiply $\sum p_kz^k$ by $1-z$: the coefficients become the drops $p_{k-1}-p_k$, and at $|z|=1$ the triangle inequality is an equality only if every drop sits at a power with $z^k=1$ (Hurwitz 1913).

A lower bound from an integral with positive weight

$\mathrm{Li}_s(z)/z=\int_0^1d\mu_s(t)/(1-tz)$ with $d\mu_s=(-\ln t)^{s-1}dt/\Gamma(s)$, and $\mathrm{Re}\,\frac1{1-tz}-\frac1{1+t}=\frac{t(1+\cos\theta)(1-t)}{(1+t)|1-tz|^2}\ge0$ on the circle; integrate, then $r\to1^-$ by Abel’s theorem. The same lines work for any coefficients that are moments of a positive measure on $[0,1)$.

Key ideas and tricks (5)

Two accidents of the golden ratio

From $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$: $1/\varphi+1/\varphi^2=1$ (a reflection pair) and $\frac{1/\varphi^2}{1/\varphi^2-1}=-1/\varphi$ (Landen sends $1/\varphi^2$ to $-1/\varphi$, and $1/\varphi^2\approx0.382$ lies inside the proven Landen domain).
↳ Theorem 1
📄 from the paper §3

The circle was measuring time

A sum $\sum p_ne^{-in\theta}$ with positive weights is the autocorrelation of a quantum state with populations $p_n$ on equally spaced levels. The positivity of the Clausen function then says that the weight-2 zeta state never becomes orthogonal: the dilogarithm thread and the speed-limit thread cross here.
↳ Theorem 4
📄 from the paper §5 («The turn») and §6

A dominant level stops the tick, not the evolution

The zeta state never becomes orthogonal because more than half of it sits on one level, not because its energy sits in a heavy tail (the tail only makes the moments infinite). A dominant level forbids exact orthogonality, not fast evolution: $\sqrt{0.6}\,|0\rangle+\sqrt{0.4}\,|\Omega\rangle$ never ticks but reaches its smallest overlap at $t=\pi/\Omega$.
↳ Theorem 4
📄 from the paper §6

The moment-based limits see one number

As their order tends to zero, the Zieliński–Zych limits keep only the population of the lowest level: they certify $\tau_\perp=\infty$ exactly when it exceeds $\tfrac12$. The exact floor of Theorem 5 sees the shape of the populations instead.
↳ Proposition 1, Corollary 1
📄 from the paper §6 and §7

Check yourself (5)

1.

Show by hand that $\sin\theta+\tfrac12\sin2\theta>0$ on $(0,\pi)$ (Theorem 2 with $M=2$).
answerIt equals $\sin\theta(1+\cos\theta)$, and both factors are positive on $(0,\pi)$.
↳ Theorem 2

2.

Using only $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$, check that $1/\varphi+1/\varphi^2=1$ and that Landen's map $x\mapsto x/(x-1)$ sends $1/\varphi^2$ to $-1/\varphi$.
answer$1/\varphi+1/\varphi^2=(\varphi+1)/\varphi^2=1$. And $\frac{1/\varphi^2}{1/\varphi^2-1}=\frac{1}{1-\varphi^2}=\frac{1}{-\varphi}$.
↳ Theorem 1

3.

Compute the overlap $S(\pi)$ of the weight-2 zeta state, $S(\theta)=\frac6{\pi^2}\mathrm{Li}_2(e^{-i\theta})$, and the survival probability there.
answer$\mathrm{Li}_2(-1)=-\pi^2/12$, so $S(\pi)=-\tfrac12$ and the survival probability $|S(\pi)|^2=\tfrac14$: that is the floor.
↳ Theorem 4

4.

The two-level state $(|0\rangle+|E\rangle)/\sqrt2$: when is it first orthogonal to itself, and how does that compare with Margolus–Levitin?
answer$S(t)=\tfrac12(1+e^{-iEt})$ vanishes first at $t=\pi/E$. Its mean energy is $E/2$, and Margolus–Levitin gives $\tau\ge\pi/(2\langle E\rangle)=\pi/E$: the bound is reached.
↳ Theorem 3

5.

For which $s$ does the lowest level of the zeta state carry more than half of the weight?
answerWhen $1/\zeta(s)>\tfrac12$, that is $\zeta(s)<2$, that is $s>s^*\approx1.7286$. Below $s^*$ the one-line argument fails and Corollary 1 is needed.
↳ Corollary 1
