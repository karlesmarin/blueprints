The Clock That Never Ticks: a machine-checked path from the dilogarithm to quantum speed limits in Lean 4

🏷️ Special functions · the dilogarithm and the Clausen function

Carles Marín

At a glance

What the paper contributes

A sorry-free Lean 4 path from the dilogarithm, which Mathlib cites but does not define, to a quantum state that never orthogonalizes; version 2 adds its exact floor, in Lean and in Isabelle.

  • The real dilogarithm with Euler’s reflection, Landen’s transformation, the duplication formula and Li₂(1/2) (Theorem 1); the golden-ratio values and the Lee–Yang ceff = 2/5 from a 3×3 linear system, without the five-term relation.
  • The circle: the Clausen function and Catalan’s constant G = Cl₂(π/2); the Fejér–Jackson inequality (Theorem 2) and, by Abel summation without asymptotics, Cl₂(θ) ≥ sin(θ)/2 on (0, π).
  • Speed limits: Margolus–Levitin and an L¹ form of Mandelstam–Tamm for a scalar spectral measure (Theorem 3).
  • The assembly (Theorem 4): populations 6/(π²n²) on levels Eₙ = n have divergent first and second energy moments, so both bounds give nothing, and yet the state never reaches an orthogonal state. Version 2 gives the one-line reason (the lowest level carries more than half of the weight) and shows that the Zieliński–Zych limits see exactly that (Proposition 1).
  • The exact floor (Theorem 5, Corollary 1): |S| ≥ 1 − 21−s for every zeta state pₙ ∝ n−s, also where no level dominates.
  • The mathematics is classical; the paper claims the formalization, the assembly and the place of the zeta state among the speed limits.

(§3) In the Rogers normalization \(\operatorname{L}(x)={\mathrm{Li}_2}(x)+\tfrac 12\ln x\ln (1-x)\) the \(\ln ^2\) defects cancel. (§4) On the circle the dilogarithm splits into a tame half and a wild half:

\[ {\mathrm{Li}_2}(e^{i\theta }) \; =\; \underbrace{\sum _{n\ge 1}\frac{\cos n\theta }{n^2}}_{\textstyle =\; \frac{\pi ^2}{6}-\frac{\pi \theta }{2}+\frac{\theta ^2}{4}} \; +\; i\, \underbrace{\sum _{n\ge 1}\frac{\sin n\theta }{n^2}}_{\textstyle =\; {\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )} \qquad (\theta \in [0,2\pi ]). \]

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

The dilogarithm is the series \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(x)=\sum _{n\ge 1}x^n/n^2\). Its value at a point says little by itself, but its values at related points are tied by exact identities: the value at \(x\) and at \(1-x\) (Euler’s reflection), at \(x\) and at \(x/(x-1)\) (Landen), at \(x\), \(-x\) and \(x^2\) (duplication). Each identity is proved the same way: differentiate the combination, see that the derivative is zero, so the combination is constant, and find the constant as a limit at the edge of the interval. The duplication needs no calculus: it is the series split into even and odd terms. Put \(x=\tfrac 12\) in the reflection and the two sides coincide, which gives \({\mathrm{Li}_2}(\tfrac 12)\). Section 3 of the paper then uses the three identities at the golden-ratio points as three linear equations, which give the golden values without Abel’s five-term relation. The domains are the ones of the series: Landen stops at \(x=\tfrac 12\) because past it \(x/(x-1)\) leaves the disc where the series converges.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Add the waves \(\sin \theta \), \(\tfrac 12\sin 2\theta \), \(\tfrac 13\sin 3\theta \), …, stopping anywhere: between \(0\) and \(\pi \) the total is always positive. For two terms one can see it by hand, \(\sin \theta +\tfrac 12\sin 2\theta =\sin \theta \, (1+\cos \theta )\). For any number of terms the proof looks at the lowest point of the sum inside \((0,\pi )\): there the derivative vanishes, that equation factors, and either way the last wave is not negative at that point, so the minimum is at least the previous sum, positive by induction. The paper then uses these positive sums to bound the Clausen function \({\mathrm{Cl}_2}(\theta )=\sum \sin (n\theta )/n^2\): it is at least \(\sin (\theta )/2\) on \((0,\pi )\), by a summation by parts whose every term is positive, with no estimate of the tail. At \(\theta =\pi /2\) the Clausen function is Catalan’s constant \(G\approx 0.9160\).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

A quantum state that has evolved for a time \(\tau \) is fully distinguishable from where it started exactly when its autocorrelation \(S(\tau )=\sum _np_ne^{-iE_n\tau }\) is zero. The two speed limits say it cannot happen too soon. Margolus and Levitin, with energies measured from a ground level \(E_n\ge 0\): the time is at least \(\pi /(2\langle E\rangle )\), so a small mean energy forces a slow state. The second bound uses the mean absolute deviation of the energy \(D_1\): the time is at least \(1/D_1\); since \(D_1\le \Delta E\), it gives the textbook weak Mandelstam–Tamm bound \(\tau \ge 1/\Delta E\) (the sharp constant \(\pi /2\) is left open). Both are stated for the autocorrelation of a list of weights and energies, with no Hilbert space. The key step of the first is the inequality \(\cos x\ge 1-\tfrac 2\pi (x+\sin x)\) for \(x\ge 0\). Both bounds say nothing when the mean energy or the spread is infinite, which is the case of Theorem 4.

\[ \operatorname {Re}\frac{1}{1-tz}-\frac{1}{1+t} =\frac{t\, (1+\cos \theta )(1-t)}{(1+t)\, \lvert 1-tz\rvert ^{2}}\; \ge \; 0 . \]

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Give the levels \(n=1,2,3,\dots \) weights proportional to \(n^{-s}\), for any \(s{\gt}1\). Then the overlap of the state with its starting point never drops below \(1-2^{1-s}\), and it reaches that value at half period. For \(s=2\) the floor is \(\tfrac 12\). The point is the range \(1{\lt}s{\lt}1.7286\ldots \): there no level carries more than half of the weight, the speed limits built from energy moments certify nothing, and yet the floor holds.

Strategy

A chain of five questions, Q1 to Q5, each section closing on the next (paper, «How to read this paper»): what the series knows about its derivative, whether the golden ladder can be climbed without Abel's relation, what happens on the circle, why the wild half is positive, and what physical thing beats to this function. Techniques (10)

A functional equation in three moves

To prove that a combination $F$ of dilogarithms and logarithms is constant: (1) differentiate term by term and check $F^\prime=0$; (2) conclude $F$ is constant by the mean value theorem on subintervals; (3) find the constant as a limit at the edge of the interval.
🔁 also used in: The Pentagon, Machine-Checked (the five-term relation, same argument in two variables)
↳ Theorem 1
📄 from the paper §2 (Recipe box)

Replace a two-variable identity by one-variable ones at an orbit intersection

The golden values come from reflection at $1/\varphi^2$, Landen at $1/\varphi^2$ and duplication at $1/\varphi$: three linear equations in three unknowns, closed by linarith. Not a new route (the values are Landen’s, older than Abel’s relation), but one the formalization makes explicit.
↳ Theorem 1
📄 from the paper §3 and «Questions to take with you», Q2

Split a series into even and odd terms

The duplication formula $\mathrm{Li}_2(x^2)=2(\mathrm{Li}_2(x)+\mathrm{Li}_2(-x))$, Catalan's constant $G=\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/2)$ and $\eta(s)=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$ are all a rearrangement by parity (tsum_even_add_odd), with no calculus.
↳ Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Corollary 1
📄 from the paper §2, §4 and §7

Both ends at zero, the critical equation at an interior extremum

At an interior minimum of the partial sum the critical equation factors, and either branch makes the last term nonnegative there; so the minimum is at least the previous sum, positive by induction. The same shape proves two positivity statements of the development: Fejér–Jackson and the cosine inequality of Margolus–Levitin; $\mathrm{Cl}_2>0$ is inherited from Fejér–Jackson by Abel summation.
↳ Theorem 2, Theorem 3
📄 from the paper §5, §6 and Q4

A telescope without division

The Dirichlet kernel handled multiplicatively, $2\sin\tfrac\theta2\sum_{k\le M}\cos k\theta=\sin((M+\tfrac12)\theta)-\sin\tfrac\theta2$: no quotient, no removable singularity to treat in Lean.
↳ Theorem 2
📄 from the paper §5 and §8(ii)

Abel summation that keeps its first term as the bound

Writing the partial sums of $\mathrm{Cl}_2$ through the Fejér–Jackson sums, every term is positive and the $k=1$ term alone is $\sin(\theta)/2$: the bound $\mathrm{Cl}_2(\theta)\ge\sin(\theta)/2$ with no tail estimate and no asymptotics.
↳ Theorem 2
📄 from the paper §5 and §8(ii)

State the physics as a statement about numbers

The speed limits are stated for the autocorrelation of a discrete spectral measure (weights $p_n\ge0$, energies $E_n$); the Hilbert space is bookkeeping, and Margolus–Levitin needs no complex numbers.
↳ Theorem 3, Theorem 4, Proposition 1
📄 from the paper §6 and §8(ii)

One level against all the others (the triangle inequality)

If one population exceeds $\tfrac12$, the others cannot cancel it: $|S|\ge2p_j-1$ at every time. It needs no moment of the energy, so it works where the textbook limits fail.
↳ Theorem 4
📄 from the paper §6 («Why, in one line»; Uffink, after Andrews 2007)

Read a zero on the circle from the coefficients

Multiply $\sum p_kz^k$ by $1-z$: the coefficients become the drops $p_{k-1}-p_k$, and at $|z|=1$ the triangle inequality is an equality only if every drop sits at a power with $z^k=1$ (Hurwitz 1913).
↳ Corollary 1
📄 from the paper §7

A lower bound from an integral with positive weight

$\mathrm{Li}_s(z)/z=\int_0^1d\mu_s(t)/(1-tz)$ with $d\mu_s=(-\ln t)^{s-1}dt/\Gamma(s)$, and $\mathrm{Re}\,\frac1{1-tz}-\frac1{1+t}=\frac{t(1+\cos\theta)(1-t)}{(1+t)|1-tz|^2}\ge0$ on the circle; integrate, then $r\to1^-$ by Abel’s theorem. The same lines work for any coefficients that are moments of a positive measure on $[0,1)$.
↳ Theorem 5, Corollary 1

Key ideas and tricks (5)

Two accidents of the golden ratio

From $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$: $1/\varphi+1/\varphi^2=1$ (a reflection pair) and $\frac{1/\varphi^2}{1/\varphi^2-1}=-1/\varphi$ (Landen sends $1/\varphi^2$ to $-1/\varphi$, and $1/\varphi^2\approx0.382$ lies inside the proven Landen domain).
↳ Theorem 1
📄 from the paper §3

The circle was measuring time

A sum $\sum p_ne^{-in\theta}$ with positive weights is the autocorrelation of a quantum state with populations $p_n$ on equally spaced levels. The positivity of the Clausen function then says that the weight-2 zeta state never becomes orthogonal: the dilogarithm thread and the speed-limit thread cross here.
↳ Theorem 4
📄 from the paper §5 («The turn») and §6

A dominant level stops the tick, not the evolution

The zeta state never becomes orthogonal because more than half of it sits on one level, not because its energy sits in a heavy tail (the tail only makes the moments infinite). A dominant level forbids exact orthogonality, not fast evolution: $\sqrt{0.6}\,|0\rangle+\sqrt{0.4}\,|\Omega\rangle$ never ticks but reaches its smallest overlap at $t=\pi/\Omega$.
↳ Theorem 4
📄 from the paper §6

The moment-based limits see one number

As their order tends to zero, the Zieliński–Zych limits keep only the population of the lowest level: they certify $\tau_\perp=\infty$ exactly when it exceeds $\tfrac12$. The exact floor of Theorem 5 sees the shape of the populations instead.
↳ Proposition 1, Corollary 1
📄 from the paper §6 and §7

The library already had half of the answer

On the circle the real part of $\mathrm{Li}_2(e^{i\theta})$ is a parabola, which is the Fourier series of the Bernoulli polynomial $B_2$ already in Mathlib (hasSum_one_div_nat_pow_mul_cos); only the imaginary half, the Clausen function, had to be built.

1.

Show by hand that $\sin\theta+\tfrac12\sin2\theta>0$ on $(0,\pi)$ (Theorem 2 with $M=2$).

2.

Using only $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$, check that $1/\varphi+1/\varphi^2=1$ and that Landen's map $x\mapsto x/(x-1)$ sends $1/\varphi^2$ to $-1/\varphi$.
answer$1/\varphi+1/\varphi^2=(\varphi+1)/\varphi^2=1$. And $\frac{1/\varphi^2}{1/\varphi^2-1}=\frac{1}{1-\varphi^2}=\frac{1}{-\varphi}$.

3.

Compute the overlap $S(\pi)$ of the weight-2 zeta state, $S(\theta)=\frac6{\pi^2}\mathrm{Li}_2(e^{-i\theta})$, and the survival probability there.
answer$\mathrm{Li}_2(-1)=-\pi^2/12$, so $S(\pi)=-\tfrac12$ and the survival probability $|S(\pi)|^2=\tfrac14$: that is the floor.

4.

The two-level state $(|0\rangle+|E\rangle)/\sqrt2$: when is it first orthogonal to itself, and how does that compare with Margolus–Levitin?
answer$S(t)=\tfrac12(1+e^{-iEt})$ vanishes first at $t=\pi/E$. Its mean energy is $E/2$, and Margolus–Levitin gives $\tau\ge\pi/(2\langle E\rangle)=\pi/E$: the bound is reached.
