Quadratic persistence of Segre varieties and a linear gap between biquadratic SOS rank and Zarankiewicz bounds

🏷️ Real algebraic geometry · sums of squares on projective varieties

Carles Marín

At a glance

What the paper contributes

The quadratic persistence of the Segre varieties $\mathbb P^{m-1}\times\mathbb P^2$ and $\mathbb P^{m-1}\times\mathbb P^3$, exactly, and a block bound in every width. Through the Pythagoras-number bound of Blekherman, Sinn, Smith and Velasco this shows that the sum-of-squares rank $\mathrm{BSR}(m,n)$ of biquadratic forms is not captured by the graph parameters $z_2$, $z_{2A}$ used to bound it: the difference grows linearly in $m$ in every width $n\ge4$.

  • $\mathrm{qp}(\mathbb P^{m-1}\times\mathbb P^2)=m$ and $\mathrm{qp}(\mathbb P^{m-1}\times\mathbb P^3)=\lceil4m/3\rceil$ (Theorems 1 and 2); $\mathrm{qp}\le n\lceil m/3\rceil$ (Theorem 3).
  • $\mathrm{BSR}(m,4)\ge\lfloor8m/3\rfloor$, above $z_2(m,4)=z_{2A}(m,4)$ for $m=21$ and $m\ge23$ (Corollary 4); a gap of order $\frac{n-3}6m$ in width $n$ (Corollary 5).
  • Rational forms whose rational length exceeds their real length by exactly three (Theorem 6).
  • Exact persistence in widths five and six for $m\le18$, $m\le14$ (Proposition 7), and $8\le\mathrm{BSR}(4,3)\le9$ (Theorem 9).

📐 Setting (copied from the paper, §2)

Let \(A=\mathbb {R}^m\), \(B=\mathbb {R}^n\) and \(X=\mathbb {P}(A)\times \mathbb {P}(B)\) in \(\mathbb {P}(A\otimes B)\). Let \(\mathcal D\) be the space of symmetric bilinear forms \(D\) on \(A\otimes B\) with \(D(z,z)=0\) for every simple tensor \(z\). It is spanned by the \(2\times 2\) minors, and identifies with \(\bigwedge ^2A^*\otimes \bigwedge ^2B^*\): we write \(D(a,a';b,b')\), alternating in \((a,a')\) and in \((b,b')\), and \(d=\dim \mathcal D=\binom m2\binom n2\). A point \(a\otimes b\) lies in the radical of \(D\) if and only if \(D(a,x;b,y)=0\) for all \(x\in A\), \(y\in B\). … \(\mathrm{qp}(X)\) is the least \(k\) such that some \(k\) points of \(X\) lie in the radical of no nonzero \(D\in \mathcal D\), and general points realize it. We use the bound

\[ d\le g(s):=sc-\binom s2,\qquad s=\mathrm{qp}(X),\ c=(m-1)(n-1). \]

(§1) A biquadratic form \(f(x,y)\), \(x\in \mathbb {R}^m\), \(y\in \mathbb {R}^n\), is a sum of squares if \(f=\sum _{k\le r}b_k^2\) with \(b_k\) bilinear; let \(\ell _K(f)\) be the least such \(r\) with \(b_k\) over a field \(K\). For \(m,n\ge 2\) the invariant \(\mathrm{BSR}(m,n)\) is the maximum, over real sum-of-squares biquadratic forms of size \(m\times n\), of \(\ell _{\mathbb R}(f)\).

↳ Used in: Theorem 2, Theorem 3, Corollary 4

Proof ▼

Take \(b_i=(1,i,i^2)\) and the points \(e_i\otimes b_i\). If they lie in the radical of \(D\), then \(D_{ij}\) has \(b_i\) and \(b_j\) in its radical. These are independent, and a nonzero alternating form on \({\mathbb {R}}^3\) has a radical of dimension one, so \(D_{ij}=0\) and \(D=0\). Thus \(\operatorname{qp}\le m\). Here \(c=2(m-1)\) and \(d=3\binom m2\), and \(d-g(m-1)=m-1{\gt}0\), so (1) excludes \(s\le m-1\). This lower bound was known: \(\operatorname{qp}(X)\ge \ell (X)\) [ 1 , Theorem 1.2 ] , and the linear strand of a Segre product is nonzero up to \(p=m+n-3\) [ 6 , Theorem 4.1 ] , so \(\operatorname{qp}\ge m+n-3\); what is new is the equality.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Quadratic persistence asks how many points of a variety one has to pick so that no quadric through the variety is singular at all of them. For the Segre variety of \(m\times 3\) matrices of rank one the answer is exactly \(m\). Upper bound: put one point in each row, \(e_i\otimes (1,i,i^2)\); a quadric singular at two such points restricts, for each pair of rows, to an alternating form on \(\mathbb R^3\) with two independent vectors in its radical, hence zero. Lower bound: the dimension count of Blekherman, Sinn, Smith and Velasco excludes \(m-1\) points; it was already known, also from the linear strand of Oeding, Raicu and Sam, so what the note adds is the equality.

Proof ▼

Upper bound. Write \(B=E\oplus \langle w\rangle \) with \(\dim E=3\), split \(\{ 1,\dots ,m\} \) into consecutive groups of three, the last possibly shorter, and in a basis of \(E\) put \(b_i=(1,i,i^3)\). Any three \(b_i\) are independent, and any six of the tensors \(b_i\otimes b_i\) are independent: their coordinates are the monomials \(1,t,t^3\), respectively \(1,t,t^2,t^3,t^4,t^6\), at distinct positive nodes, and a nonzero combination of \(k\) monomials has at most \(k-1\) positive roots by Descartes’ rule. Take the \(m+\lceil m/3\rceil =\lceil 4m/3\rceil \) integer points

\[ e_i\otimes b_i\quad (1\le i\le m),\qquad \Bigl(\sum _{j\in S}e_j\Bigr)\otimes w\quad \text{for each group }S. \]

Suppose they lie in the radical of \(D\). As above, \(D_{ij}\) has \(b_i,b_j\) in its radical, so \(D_{ij}\) factors through the plane \(B/\langle b_i,b_j\rangle \) and \(D_{ij}=c_{ij}\operatorname {vol}(b_i,b_j,\cdot ,\cdot )\) for a fixed volume form, with \(c_{ij}=c_{ji}\) and \(c_{ii}=0\). The point of the group \(S\) gives, for every \(i\) and \(y\), \(\sum _{j\in S}c_{ij}\operatorname {vol}(b_i,b_j,w,y)=0\), that is,

\begin{equation} \sum _{j\in S}c_{ij}b_j\in \langle b_i\rangle , \end{equation}
1

since \(b_i\) and the sum lie in \(E\) and \(w\notin E\). Inside a group, (2) and the independence of its vectors give \(c_{ij}=0\). For two groups \(U\ne V\) put \(T=\sum _{i\in U,j\in V}c_{ij}\, b_i\otimes b_j\). By (2) for \(i\in U\) and \(S=V\), \(T=\sum _{i\in U}\lambda _i\, b_i\otimes b_i\); by (2) for \(j\in V\) and \(S=U\), with \(c_{ij}=c_{ji}\), \(T=\sum _{j\in V}\mu _j\, b_j\otimes b_j\). The at most six tensors \(b_k\otimes b_k\), \(k\in U\cup V\), are independent, so \(T=0\), and since the \(b_i\otimes b_j\) with \(i\in U\), \(j\in V\) are independent, all \(c_{ij}\) vanish. Hence \(D=0\).

Lower bound. For \(m=2,3\) the claim is \(\operatorname{qp}=3,4\), which follows from \({\mathbb {P}}^1\times {\mathbb {P}}^3\), a rational normal scroll, so of minimal degree [ 1 , Theorem 3.8 ] , and Theorem 1 by symmetry. Let \(m\ge 4\) and \(k=\lceil 4m/3\rceil -1\), so \(m{\lt}k\) and \(3k{\lt}4m\). For general points \(a_i\otimes b_i\), \(i\le k\), the conditions \(Ma_i\in \langle b_i\rangle \) on \(M\colon A\to B\) are at most \(3k\) linear equations in \(4m\) unknowns, so there is \(M\ne 0\). If \(\operatorname{rank}M=r{\lt}4\), at most \(m-r\) of the general \(a_i\) lie in \(\ker M\), and for the other indices \(b_i\in \operatorname {im}M\), which allows at most \(r\) of the general \(b_i\); so \(k\le m\), a contradiction. Thus \(M\) is onto, and \(L(a\otimes b)=Ma\wedge b\) maps \(A\otimes B\) onto \(\bigwedge ^2B\), as decomposable vectors span it. Fix \(\bigwedge ^4B\cong {\mathbb {R}}\) and put \(Q(z,z')=L(z)\wedge L(z')\). It is nonzero, since the Plücker form on \(\bigwedge ^2B\) is nondegenerate, it vanishes on simple tensors because \(Ma\wedge b\) is decomposable, so \(Q\in \mathcal D\), and \(L(a_i\otimes b_i)=0\) puts the \(k\) points in its radical. So \(k\) general points do not suffice. The bound \(m+n-3=m+1\) from [ 1 , 6 ] is weaker for \(m\ge 4\), which is why a direct argument is needed.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

For \(m\times 4\) the answer is \(\lceil 4m/3\rceil \): one point per row plus one more point for every group of three rows. The extra point of a group lies in the fourth direction \(w\) and forces linear relations between the coefficients \(c_{ij}\) that remain after the row points; inside a group they vanish at once, and between two groups the symmetric tensor \(T=\sum c_{ij}b_i\otimes b_j\) would lie in two spaces of squares that meet only in \(0\), because any six of the tensors \(b_i\otimes b_i\), with \(b_i=(1,i,i^3)\), are independent (Descartes’ rule of signs). The points are integers, so the bound needs no genericity. Fewer points never suffice: with \(\lceil 4m/3\rceil -1\) general points there is a nonzero linear map \(M\) with \(Ma_i\in \langle b_i\rangle \); it must be onto, and the Plücker quadric pulled back through \(a\otimes b\mapsto Ma\wedge b\) is singular at all the points.

Proof ▼

By symmetry it suffices to prove \(\operatorname{qp}\le sn\) for \(m=3s\); for other \(m\), project \({\mathbb {R}}^{3\lceil m/3\rceil }\) onto \(A\): if \(\pi \) is onto, \(\pi (a)\otimes b\) is in the radical of \(D\) exactly when \(a\otimes b\) is in the radical of \(\pi ^*D\), and \(\pi ^*\) is injective. Split \(A=U_1\oplus \dots \oplus U_s\) with \(\dim U_l=3\) and put \(n\) points in each block, \(a^{(l)}_t\otimes b^{(l)}_t\) with \(a^{(l)}_t\in U_l\). Since \(\bigwedge ^2A^*=\bigoplus _l\bigwedge ^2U_l^*\oplus \bigoplus _{l{\lt}l'}U_l^*\otimes U_{l'}^*\), and the radical conditions of the points of \(U_l\) only involve the components containing \(U_l^*\), the map \(D\mapsto \)(radical conditions) is injective as soon as it is injective on each internal component \(\bigwedge ^2U_l^*\otimes \bigwedge ^2B^*\), using the points of \(U_l\), and on each mixed component \(U_l^*\otimes U_{l'}^*\otimes \bigwedge ^2B^*\), using the points of \(U_l\) and \(U_{l'}\). Each of these is a nonempty Zariski open condition once it holds for one choice of points, so it suffices to exhibit one choice per component.

Internal. Take points \(a_t\otimes b_t\) with \(b_1,\dots ,b_n\) a basis of \(B\) and any three \(a_t\) independent. In the dual basis write \(D=\sum _{t{\lt}u}D^{tu}\otimes (b_t^*\wedge b_u^*)\) with \(D^{tu}\in \bigwedge ^2U^*\). The point \(a_t\otimes b_t\) forces \(D^{tu}(a_t,\cdot )=0\) for all \(u\), so \(D^{tu}\) has \(a_t,a_u\) in its radical and vanishes, as in Theorem 1.

Mixed. Let \(U,V\) be two blocks. In \(U\) take \(a_i\otimes e_i\), \(i\in \mathbb Z/n\), for a basis \(e_i\) of \(B\) and any three \(a_i\) independent; in \(V\), over \(\mathbb C\), take \(c_t\otimes f_t\) with \(c_t=(1,t,t^2)\) and \(f_t=\sum _it^ie_i\), for the \(n\)-th roots of unity \(t\). Write the component as \(\sum _{i{\lt}j}M^{ij}\otimes (e_i^*\wedge e_j^*)\) with \(M^{ij}\) bilinear on \(U\times V\) and \(M^{ji}=-M^{ij}\). The points of \(U\) give \(M^{ij}(a_i,\cdot )=M^{ij}(a_j,\cdot )=0\), so \(M^{ij}=(a_i\times a_j)\otimes v_{ij}\), where \(a_i\times a_j\) is the covector \(x\mapsto \det (a_i,a_j,x)\) and \(v_{ij}=v_{ji}\in V^*\). Write \(v_{ij}(1,t,t^2)=\sum _{h=0}^2c_{ijh}t^h\). The points of \(V\) give, for each \(j\) and each \(t\), \(\sum _{i\ne j}\sum _hc_{ijh}t^{i+h}(a_i\times a_j)=0\); as \(t\) runs over the \(n\)-th roots of unity, the coefficient of each power \(t^p\) modulo \(t^n-1\) vanishes:

\begin{equation} \sum _{h=0}^2c_{p-h,j,h}\, (a_{p-h}\times a_j)=0\qquad (j,p\in \mathbb Z/n,\ p-h\ne j). \end{equation}
2

The unknown \(c_{ijh}=c_{jih}\) appears only in the equations with \(i+j+h=\lambda \) fixed, so the system splits by \(\lambda \). For fixed \(\lambda \) consider the graph on \(\mathbb Z/n\) with an edge \(\{ i,\lambda -h-i\} \) for each \(h\in \{ 0,1,2\} \) and each \(i\), loops removed; the edge carries \(c_{i,\lambda -h-i,h}\), and (3) at the vertex \(j\) involves exactly its incident edges, with pairwise independent covectors \(a_k\times a_j\). So at a vertex where at most two unknowns are not yet known to vanish, all of them vanish. The reflections \(i\mapsto \lambda -h-i\) for \(h=0,1\) compose to \(i\mapsto i+1\), so the graph is connected, and \(2i\equiv \lambda -h\) has a solution for some \(h\in \{ 0,1\} \), which gives a vertex with a removed loop and at most two edges. Starting there and moving along edges, every vertex has at most two unknowns left, so all \(c_{ijh}\) vanish. The relevant minors are therefore nonzero polynomials, and general rational points satisfy all the finitely many open conditions at once.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

In any width \(n\ge 3\), cut the \(m\) rows into blocks of three and put \(n\) points in each block: at most \(n\lceil m/3\rceil \) points. The quadrics split into pieces living on one block or on a pair of blocks, and the points of a block only see the pieces that contain it, so it is enough to kill each piece with one good choice of points. Inside a block the alternating argument of Theorem 1 works. For a pair of blocks, a second block with points \((1,t,t^2)\otimes (1,t,\dots ,t^{n-1})\) at the \(n\)-th roots of unity turns the conditions into a system that splits by \(i+j+h\); each piece is a connected cycle-like graph with a vertex of degree two, and the unknowns vanish one after another along it. This is only an upper bound for \(\mathrm{qp}\), but it is enough for a gap that grows linearly in \(m\) (Corollary 5).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

\(\mathrm{BSR}(m,n)\) is the largest number of squares a sum of squares of bilinear forms can need. Blekherman, Sinn, Smith and Velasco turn persistence into a lower bound: \(\mathrm{BSR}\ge mn-\mathrm{qp}\). With Theorem 2 this gives \(\mathrm{BSR}(m,4)\ge \lfloor 8m/3\rfloor \). That \(\mathrm{BSR}\) and \(z_2\) differ somewhere was known from \(4\times 4\) (Qi and Cui); what is new is a gap that grows.

Proof ▼

There are \(k\) independent rational simple tensors \(p_i\) with \(D\mapsto (Dp_1,\dots ,Dp_k)\) injective on \(\mathcal D\), since this is a nonempty Zariski open condition and rational points are dense. The face of the forms vanishing at these points is the mechanism of [ 1 , Lemma 3.18 ] . Let \(u_1,\dots ,u_e\) be a rational basis of the annihilator of the \(p_i\), read as bilinear forms, and \(F=u_1^2+\dots +u_{e-1}^2+7u_e^2\). A positive semidefinite Gram matrix \(G\) of \(F\) has \(p_i^{\top }Gp_i=F(p_i)=0\), hence \(Gp_i=0\); the difference of two such lies in \(\mathcal D\) and kills all \(p_i\), so the positive semidefinite Gram matrix is unique, of rank \(e\). Its nondegenerate part is \((e-1)\langle 1\rangle \perp \langle 7\rangle \) over \({\mathbb {Q}}\), so a representation by \(r\) rational squares is an embedding of this form in \(r\langle 1\rangle \), and by Witt cancellation it exists exactly when \(7\) is a sum of \(r-e+1\) rational squares. Since \(7\) is a sum of four rational squares and not of three, \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(F)=e+3\).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

Over the rationals the count changes. Take the rational bilinear forms that vanish at \(\mathrm{qp}\) well-chosen points; every positive semidefinite Gram matrix of a sum of their squares must kill those points, and this pins the Gram matrix down uniquely, the face mechanism of Blekherman, Sinn, Smith and Velasco. Now weight one square by \(7\). Over \(\mathbb R\) the form still needs \(e=mn-\mathrm{qp}\) squares; over \(\mathbb Q\) a representation is an embedding of \((e-1)\langle 1\rangle \perp \langle 7\rangle \) into a sum of unit squares, and by Witt cancellation it needs \(7\) as a sum of rational squares, which takes four, not three. So exactly three more squares.

Proof ▼

Let \(m\ge n\) and \(f=\sum _kb_k^2\); let \(v_{ij}\in {\mathbb {R}}^r\) be the coefficient vector of \(x_iy_j\) in \((b_1,\dots ,b_r)\). The vectors \(v_{ij}\) with \(j\ge 3\) span a space \(W\) of dimension at most \(m(n-2)\). After an orthogonal change of the \(r\) coordinates, \(W\) lies in the first \(\dim W\) of them, and the remaining coordinates give bilinear forms in \(x\) and \(y_1,y_2\) only. Their sum of squares is a form of size \(m\times 2\), hence a sum of \(m+1\) squares [ 1 , Theorem 1.4 ] . So \(f\) is a sum of \(m(n-2)+m+1\) squares. For \(m{\lt}n\) exchange the roles of \(x\) and \(y\). The lower bound for \(n=3\) is Corollary 4.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

From above: keep two columns \(y_1,y_2\). The coefficient vectors of the other monomials span at most \(m(n-2)\) directions; rotate them into the first coordinates, and what is left involves only \(y_1,y_2\), a form of size \(m\times 2\), which is a sum of \(m+1\) squares. So \(mn-\max (m,n)+1\) squares always suffice. This is a covering bound of Blekherman, Sinn, Smith and Velasco (Corollary 2.15) read for the product of simplices; the note gives the direct proof and the consequence \(8\le \mathrm{BSR}(4,3)\le 9\), against the bound \(11\) used before.
