1 · The result

Theorem 1.

For every \(m\ge 3\) with \(m\equiv 0\) or \(3\pmod4\) there is a completely multiplicative \(\varepsilon :\mathbb N\to \{ \pm 1\} \) with

\[ { \boxed {\displaystyle \sum _{n\le m}n\, \varepsilon (n)=0.}} \]

2 · In plain words

\paragraph{In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper).} Changing one sign changes $S_\varepsilon(m)$ by an even amount, so a zero is only possible at the admissible lengths $m\equiv0,3\pmod4$; the theorem says that parity is the only obstruction. A prime $q\in(m/2,m]$ divides no other $n\le m$, so its sign enters the sum once, with weight $q$. Choose the other signs so that the rest of the sum is small (Lemma \ref{lem:momento}), and show that the subset sums of these large primes fill a long interval (Lemma \ref{lem:intervalos}); then the large primes cancel the rest exactly. The function depends on $m$. The proof runs in three ranges (Table 1 of the paper), which overlap on purpose: explicit functions for $3\le m\le20000$, $104$ block certificates on $[5000,10^8)$, and S\'ark\"ozy's progressions from $10^8$ on; the last is available from $M\approx2.892\cdot10^7$ (natural logarithm in Lemma \ref{lem:intervalos}(c); about $4.4\cdot10^7$ if it is read to base $2$), so the three ranges cover every $m\ge3$ with room.

3 · How it is proved

What the paper contributes

For a completely multiplicative ε : ℕ → {±1}, the weighted sum Sε(m) = Σn≤m n ε(n) can vanish only when m ≡ 0, 3 (mod 4).

  • Every admissible length is a zero (Theorem 1): for every such m ≥ 3 some ε has Sε(m) = 0 — a second moment, 104 integer certificates up to 108, and Sárközy’s progressions in subset sums beyond.
  • One fixed function rarely vanishes, but generically does (Theorem 5): the ε with infinitely many zeros form a dense Gδ of probability zero, since every finite prefix of signs extends to a zero (Proposition 4).
  • Quadratic characters are explicit points of that set: a digit recursion in base q (Theorem 6) propagates their zeros for q ≡ 1 (mod 4), for q ≡ 7 (mod 8) through the Euler factor at 2 of the weighted half sum (Theorem 7, classical in form), for class number one and along loops of the recursion (Corollary 8, Proposition 9); for odd characters of any order at (qj − 1)/2 exactly when χ(2) = 1 (Corollary 10).
  • The digits are Conrey’s null cells: they are the cells on which Conrey’s sine series vanishes identically; positivity of a related series would exclude them for q ≡ 3 (mod 8) (Proposition 11).
  • The origin: on the diagonal of the moment matrix of the ring of character values, the vanishing components are the odd characters trivial on ⟨2⟩, and the rank defect is d/t for every odd conductor (Theorem 14, known in substance: Jager–Lenstra, Girstmair).
  • Added after publication: for q = 179 and 227, χq,1 has no zero at all (N1), by a finite certificate checked in Lean.

It rests on:

  • Additive combinatorics — arithmetic progressions in subset sums (Sárközy, with Lev's constants in the form of Bringmann–Wellnitz); complete sequences and pairs
  • Probability — a second-moment bound; the anticoncentration theorem of Sárközy and Szemerédi; Borel–Cantelli
  • Topology (Baire category) — dense G_δ sets in the product space of sign patterns
  • Algebraic number theory — generalized Bernoulli numbers B_{1,χ}, the class number formula of Jacobi and Dirichlet, Euler factors
  • Representation theory of finite abelian groups — the idempotents e_χ of ℂ[G]: the rank of a cyclic module is the number of non-vanishing Fourier components
  • Formal verification — Lean 4 + Mathlib: the digit recursion and its corollaries, the weighted half sum, the subset-sum lemmas, the parity of the rows, and the finite certificate of N1
