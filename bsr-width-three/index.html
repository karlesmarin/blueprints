For m ≥ 2, every m×3 biquadratic sum of squares is a sum of 2m squares

🏷️ Real algebraic geometry · sums of squares on projective varieties

Carles Marín

At a glance

What the paper contributes

The exact sum-of-squares rank of biquadratic forms of width three: every $m\times3$ biquadratic sum of squares is a sum of $2m$ squares of bilinear forms, and some need $2m$. This answers the question of Qi, Löfberg and Chen on the exact values of $\mathrm{BSR}(m,3)$; the cases $m=2,3$ were known, $m\ge4$ is new.

  • $\mathrm{BSR}(m,3)=2m$ for every $m\ge2$ (Theorem 1); in particular $\mathrm{BSR}(4,3)=8$.
  • An integer point where the map of $2m$ squares is a submersion, in every size (Lemma 2).
  • A rank inequality for Hankel multipliers without simple tensors: rank at least the support plus one (Lemma 5), replacing the Gorenstein classification of the boundary strategy of Blekherman, Dunbar and Sinn.
  • An explicit form needing $2m$ squares (Lemma 6).

📐 Setting (copied from the paper, §§1–2)

Write \(x=(x_1,\dots ,x_m)\), \(y=(y_1,y_2,y_3)\). A biquadratic form is a real form of bidegree \((2,2)\) in \((x,y)\), and \(\mathrm{BSR}(m,n)\) is the least \(r\) such that every biquadratic sum of squares in \(m+n\) variables is a sum of \(r\) squares of bilinear forms. Let \(A=\mathbb R^m\), \(B=\mathbb R^3\), \(V=A\otimes B\) (bilinear forms), \(R_2\) the biquadratic forms, of dimension \(N=3m(m+1)\), \(\Phi _k(h_1,\dots ,h_k)=h_1^2+\dots +h_k^2\), \(\Sigma _k=\Phi _k(V^k)\) and \(\Sigma \) the cone of sums of squares. To \(\lambda \in R_2^*\) corresponds \(H_\lambda (u,v)=\lambda (uv)\), with the Hankel symmetry \(H(x\otimes b,x'\otimes c)=H(x\otimes c,x'\otimes b)\).

Proof ▼

We prove \(\Sigma _{2m}=\Sigma \) by strong induction on \(m\ge 2\). Suppose not, and take \(F,H\) as in Proposition 3; fix a representation \(F=\sum _{i\le 2m}h_i^2\) and \(W=W_h\). By Corollary 4 and Lemma 5, \(a+1\le r\le m\); as \(H\ne 0\), \(a\ge 1\), so \(1\le a\le m-1\). Let \(A=A'\oplus A''\) orthogonally, \(e=m-a\).

Case \(e\ge 2\). Write \(w=w'+w''\) with \(w'\in A'\otimes B\), \(w''\in A''\otimes B\). Since \(A''\otimes B\) lies in the radical of \(H\) and \(W\subseteq \ker H\), the projection \(W'\) of \(W\) to \(A'\otimes B\) lies in the kernel of \(H|_{A'\otimes B}\), which has dimension \(3a-r\). An orthogonal change of the \(2m\) summands moves all the components in \(A'\otimes B\) into at most \(s=\dim W'\le 3a-r\) of them; the other \(2m-s\) summands are bilinear forms in the \(e\) variables of \(A''\), and their sum of squares is a sum of squares in \(e\times 3\) variables. As \(2\le e{\lt}m\), by induction it is a sum of \(2e\) squares. Hence \(F\) is a sum of at most \(3a-r+2(m-a)=2m+a-r\le 2m-1\) squares, against Corollary 4.

Case \(e=1\). Then \(a=m-1\) and \(r=m=a+1\), so \(W=\ker H\) and \(A''\otimes B\subseteq W\); let \(x_0\) span \(A''\). The coefficient map \({\mathbb {R}}^{2m}\to W\) of the representation is an isomorphism (Corollary 4); choosing an orthonormal basis of the preimage of \(x_0\otimes B\) and completing it, an orthogonal change of the summands gives \(h_i=x_0\otimes y_i\) \((i=1,2,3)\) for a basis \(y_1,y_2,y_3\) of \(B\). Write \(H_{jl}\) for the blocks of \(H|_{A'\otimes B}\) in this basis (symmetric, with \(H_{jl}=H_{lj}\) by (1)) and \(S=H_{11}+H_{22}+H_{33}\). For \(v_1,v_2,v_3\in A'\) let

\[ D_i=\sum _{j,p}\varepsilon _{ijp}\, v_p\otimes y_j\quad (i=1,2,3),\qquad D_i=0\quad (i{\gt}3), \]

with \(\varepsilon \) the alternating symbol. Then \(\sum _ih_iD_i=\sum _{i,j,p}\varepsilon _{ijp}(x_0\otimes y_i)(v_p\otimes y_j)=0\) by antisymmetry in \(i,j\), and \(\sum _i\varepsilon _{ijp}\varepsilon _{ilq}=\delta _{jl}\delta _{pq}-\delta _{jq}\delta _{pl}\) gives

\[ \sum _iH(D_i,D_i)=\sum _pv_p^\top Sv_p-\sum _{p,q}v_p^\top H_{pq}v_q . \]

By Proposition 3(2) this is nonnegative for all \(v\), that is \(L:=I_3\otimes S-H|_{A'\otimes B}\succeq 0\) in the block order. The three diagonal blocks of \(L\) sum to \(3S-S=2S\), so \(S\succeq 0\). If \(Sx=0\), the three nonnegative numbers \(L(x\otimes y_j,x\otimes y_j)\) sum to \(2x^\top Sx=0\), so \(L(x\otimes y_j,\cdot )=0\) and hence \(H(x\otimes y_j,\cdot )=0\) for all \(j\); by the choice of \(A'\), \(x=0\). So \(S\succ 0\). The congruence by \(\operatorname {diag}(S^{-1/2},S^{-1/2},S^{-1/2})\) turns \(H|_{A'\otimes B}\) into \(\widetilde H\) with \(\widetilde H\preceq I_{3a}\), \(\operatorname{tr}\widetilde H=\operatorname{tr}(S^{-1/2}SS^{-1/2})=a\), \(\operatorname{rank}\widetilde H=a+1\) and the same positive and negative inertia indices as \(H\), which by Proposition 3(3) has a negative eigenvalue. Then \(\widetilde H\) has at most \(a\) positive eigenvalues, each at most \(1\), and a negative one, so \(\operatorname{tr}\widetilde H{\lt}a\): a contradiction.

For \(m=2\) only \(e=1\) occurs, so the induction starts without hypothesis.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

1. A biquadratic form in \(x\in \mathbb R^m\), \(y\in \mathbb R^3\) that is a sum of squares of bilinear forms can always be written with \(2m\) squares, and some forms need all of them. Before this note one knew \(2m\le \mathrm{BSR}(m,3)\le 2m+1\); the theorem removes the last unit. 2. Why: if some form needed more, a generic form on the edge of the set of sums of \(2m\) squares (still inside the cone) would carry a linear functional, the Hankel multiplier, satisfying first and second order conditions. Lemma 5 says such a multiplier has rank larger than its support in the \(x\) variables; this either saves a square by induction on \(m\), or leaves one row outside the support, where a second variation built from the alternating symbol contradicts a trace count. 3. New and not new, as the paper says: the statement is new for \(m\ge 4\); \(m=2\) follows from [BPSV] and [BSSV, Theorem 1.4], \(m=3\) from [BSSV, Theorem 1.5]; the strategy is that of Blekherman, Dunbar and Sinn [BDS]; the question is asked in [QLC, §9]. 4. The lower bound \(2m\) comes from \(\mathrm{qp}(\mathbb P^{m-1}\times \mathbb P^2)=m\) [Mar, Theorem 1] and from the explicit form of Lemma 6.

3 A regular point and a boundary point

Lemma 2✅ Isabelle

For every \(m\ge 2\) there are \(h\in V^{2m}\) with integer coefficients at which \(d\Phi _{2m}\) is onto. In particular \(\Sigma _{2m}\) has nonempty interior.

↳ Used in: Theorem 1, Proposition 3

Proof ▼

Identify \(V\) with \(A^3\) by the three columns (\(y_1,y_2,y_3\)). Let \(P\) be the cyclic permutation matrix of size \(m\), \(D=\operatorname {diag}(1,\dots ,m)\), \(Q=DP^{-1}\) and \(C=[\, I\ \, P\ \, Q\, ]\colon A^3\to A\). Take for \(h\) the integer basis \((-Pe_i,e_i,0)\), \((-Qe_i,0,e_i)\), \(1\le i\le m\), of \(\ker C\), so \(W_h=\ker C\). By (2) we must show that a symmetric \(H\) with (1) and \(\ker C\subseteq \ker H\) vanishes. Such an \(H\) is \(C^\top MC\) with \(M\) symmetric, with blocks \(M\), \(MP\), \(MQ\), \(P^\top MP\), \(P^\top MQ\), \(Q^\top MQ\). Symmetry of the off-diagonal blocks gives \(MP=P^\top M\), \(MQ=Q^\top M\) and \(P^\top MQ=Q^\top MP\). Then \(P^\top MQ=P^\top Q^\top M\) and \(Q^\top MP=Q^\top P^\top M\), so \((PQ-QP)^\top M=0\), that is \(M(PQ-QP)=0\). Now \(PQ-QP=PDP^{-1}-D\) is diagonal with entries \(d_{\sigma (i)}-d_i=\pm 1\) or \(\pm (m-1)\), hence invertible, and \(M=0\).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

1. There is an explicit choice of \(2m\) bilinear forms with integer coefficients at which small changes of the forms move their sum of squares in every direction: the set of sums of \(2m\) squares has interior. 2. Why: a direction that no change reaches would be a symmetric matrix \(M\) commuting with \(P\) and \(Q\) in the sense \(MP=P^\top M\), \(MQ=Q^\top M\), \(P^\top MQ=Q^\top MP\), which forces \(M(PQ-QP)=0\); with \(P\) cyclic and \(Q=DP^{-1}\) the commutator is diagonal and invertible. 3. This replaces the quaternions used for \(4\times 3\) in an earlier version of the argument; it works in every size. 4. The lab computes the rank of the differential for \(2\le m\le 6\) modulo \(1000003\); the scripts check \(2\le m\le 7\), and the control \(Q=P\) loses rank.

📕 p. 3📍 map❝ cite⚠ report

Suppose \(\Sigma _{2m}\ne \Sigma \). Then there are \(F\in \Sigma _{2m}\) in the interior of \(\Sigma \) and \(\lambda \in R_2^*\setminus 0\), \(H=H_\lambda \), such that for every \(h\in V^{2m}\) with \(\Phi _{2m}(h)=F\):

  1. \(W_h\subseteq \ker H\) and \(\lambda (F)=0\);

  2. \(\sum _iH(D_i,D_i)\ge 0\) for every \(D\in V^{2m}\) with \(\sum _ih_iD_i=0\);

  3. \(H\) is indefinite;

  4. the \(h_i\) have no common zero \(([x],[y])\in X({\mathbb {C}})\).

🆕 Classical and new: the paper: «This is the argument of [BDS, Proposition 2.2 and Proposition 3.3]».

↳ Used in: Theorem 1, Corollary 4

Proof ▼

This is the argument of [ 2 , Proposition 2.2 and Proposition 3.3 ] ; we give it in our setting. Let \(\Omega \) be the interior of \(\Sigma \). The set \(\Omega \cap \Sigma _{2m}\) contains a nonempty open set (Lemma 2 and the submersion theorem), and \(\Omega \setminus \Sigma _{2m}\) is open, and nonempty because \(\Sigma \setminus \Sigma _{2m}\) is a nonempty relatively open subset of \(\Sigma =\overline\Omega \). Fix a semialgebraic stratification of \(R_2\) compatible with \(\Sigma _{2m}\) and \(\Omega \) and satisfying the frontier condition [ 1 , § 9 ] . A generic segment in the convex set \(\Omega \) joining the two open sets avoids every stratum of dimension at most \(N-2\) and meets the strata of dimension \(N-1\) transversally; where it leaves \(\Sigma _{2m}\) it crosses a stratum \(S\subseteq \partial \Sigma _{2m}\) of dimension \(N-1\) with \(\Sigma _{2m}\) on one side and its complement on the other. On a small ball \(U\) around the crossing point only \(S\) and open strata meet, each component of \(U\setminus S\) lies inside or outside \(\Sigma _{2m}\), and there is a smooth \(\rho \) on \(U\) with \(\rho =0\) on \(S\cap U\), \(d\rho \ne 0\) and \(\rho \ge 0\) on \(\Sigma _{2m}\cap U\).

We choose \(F\in S\cap U\) outside the set \(Z\) of forms in \(\Omega \) that are singular at a point of \(X({\mathbb {C}})\). A form in \(\Omega \) is positive on \(X({\mathbb {R}})\), and a singular point \(p=([x],[y])\) has no real factor: if \([x]\) is real, take a real representative \(x\ne 0\) and write \(F(x,y)=y^{\mathsf T}M_F(x)\, y\); since \(F\) is positive on \(X({\mathbb {R}})\), \(M_F(x)\) is positive definite, and singularity at \(p\) would require \(M_F(x)y=0\), impossible also over \({\mathbb {C}}\) (symmetrically if \([y]\) is real). So \(p\) and \(\bar p\) differ in both factors. After complex changes of coordinates \(p,\bar p\) become \((e_1,f_1),(e_2,f_2)\); singularity at \((e_i,f_i)\) forces to vanish, in the chart \(x_i=y_i=1\), the coefficients of \(x_i^2y_i^2\), \(x_ix_jy_i^2\) \((j\ne i)\) and \(x_i^2y_iy_l\) \((l\ne i)\), which are \(m+2\) conditions, and the two lists are disjoint. For real forms this is \(2(m+2)\) real conditions, while \(p\) moves in \(2(m+1)\) real dimensions; hence \(\dim Z\le N-2{\lt}\dim S\).

Put \(\lambda =d\rho _F\). For a preimage \(h\), \(\rho \circ \Phi _{2m}\ge 0\) near \(h\) and vanishes at \(h\), so it has a local minimum there: the first order condition is \(\lambda \circ d\Phi _{2m}(h)=0\), which is (1) by (2), and \(\lambda (F)=\sum _iH(h_i,h_i)=0\). For \(D\) in the kernel of \(d\Phi _{2m}(h)\) the second derivative of \(\rho \circ \Phi _{2m}\) along \(D\) is \(d\rho _F(d^2\Phi _{2m}[D,D])=2\sum _iH(D_i,D_i)\), which is nonnegative at a minimum: this is (2). If \(H\) or \(-H\) were positive semidefinite, \(\lambda \) or \(-\lambda \) would be nonnegative on \(\Sigma \) and vanish at the interior point \(F\), so \(\lambda =0\); this proves (3). A common complex zero of the \(h_i\) is a singular point of \(F\), excluded by \(F\notin Z\): this is (4).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

1. If not every sum of squares were a sum of \(2m\) squares, the set of sums of \(2m\) squares would have an edge inside the cone. At a generic point \(F\) of that edge there is a linear functional \(\lambda \), vanishing on the directions the map can reach and nonnegative on the second order directions; it is the Hankel multiplier \(H\). 2. Why: the edge is locally a smooth hypersurface with the sums of \(2m\) squares on one side; its defining function, composed with the map, has a minimum at every preimage of \(F\), and the first and second derivatives give (1) and (2). A semidefinite \(H\) would make \(\pm \lambda \) a positive functional vanishing at an interior point, so \(H\) is indefinite. 3. This is the argument of Blekherman, Dunbar and Sinn [BDS, Propositions 2.2 and 3.3]; the count that avoids complex base points (\(2(m+2)\) real conditions against \(2(m+1)\) parameters) is written here for \(X=\mathbb P^{m-1}\times \mathbb P^2\). 4. Not formalized: it uses semialgebraic stratification; in the planned Isabelle formalization it is the explicit hypothesis.

In the situation of Proposition 3, for every representation \(F=\sum _{i\le 2m}h_i^2\) the \(h_i\) are linearly independent, \(F\) is not a sum of fewer than \(2m\) squares, \(r:=\operatorname{rank}H\le m\), and \(\operatorname{im}H_{\mathbb {C}}\) contains no nonzero simple tensor \(x\otimes y\).

↳ Used in: Theorem 1

Proof ▼

This is [ 2 , Lemma 3.4 and Proposition 3.3(4) ] in our setting. If the \(h_i\) were dependent, an orthogonal change of the \(2m\) summands (which does not change \(F\)) would make \(h_{2m}=0\); then \(D=(0,\dots ,0,v)\) satisfies \(\sum h_iD_i=0\) for every \(v\), and Proposition 3(2) gives \(H(v,v)\ge 0\) for all \(v\), against (3). A representation with fewer squares, completed with zeros, would be dependent. So \(\dim W_h=2m\) and \(\operatorname{im}H\subseteq W_h^\perp \) has dimension at most \(3m-2m=m\). Finally \(\operatorname{im}H_{\mathbb {C}}\subseteq (W_h)_{\mathbb {C}}^\perp \), and \(x\otimes y\in (W_h)_{\mathbb {C}}^\perp \) means \(h_i(x,y)=0\) for all \(i\), excluded by (4).

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

1. At such an edge point every representation by \(2m\) squares uses \(2m\) independent forms, so the multiplier, which kills them, has rank at most \(3m-2m=m\); and its image contains no simple tensor, because that would be a common zero of the forms. 2. Why the forms are independent: otherwise a rotation would make one of them zero, and changing only that one is allowed by the first order condition, so the second order condition would make \(H\) semidefinite. 3. These are the two facts the induction uses: \(r\le m\) and no simple tensor in the image.

4 The support of a multiplier

Lemma 5

📕 p. 4📍 map❝ cite⚠ report

Let \(H\ne 0\) be symmetric with (1) and support of dimension \(a\) in the first factor. If \(\operatorname{im}H_{\mathbb {C}}\) contains no nonzero simple tensor, then \(\operatorname{rank}H\ge a+1\).

↳ Used in: Theorem 1

Proof ▼

Restrict to \(A'\otimes B\), complexify and write \(H(u,v)=J(Tu,Tv)\) with \(T\colon A'_{\mathbb {C}}\otimes B_{\mathbb {C}}\to E\) onto, \(\dim E=r=\operatorname{rank}H\), and \(J\) a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on \(E\). Put \(T_bx=T(x\otimes b)\). Trivial radical means \(\bigcap _b\ker T_b=0\), and (1) reads

\begin{equation} J(T_bx,T_cv)=J(T_cx,T_bv). \end{equation}
1

A line gives a simple tensor. If \(x\ne 0\) and \(T_bx=\beta (b)e\) for a linear form \(\beta \ne 0\) and \(e\ne 0\), choose \(c\) with \(\beta (c)=1\); by (3), \(J(e,T_bv)=\beta (b)\, J(e,T_cv)\) for all \(v\in A'\), \(b\in B\). The left side, as a linear form in \(v\otimes b\), lies in \(\operatorname{im}H_{\mathbb {C}}\) (\(T\) is onto, \(J\) nondegenerate) and is nonzero; the right side is the simple tensor \(\beta \otimes J(e,T_c\, \cdot )\). So it suffices to find such an \(x\) when \(r\le a\).

Assume \(r\le a\). Choose \(b\) with \(s=\operatorname{rank}T_b\) maximal, \(U=\operatorname{im}T_b\), \(N_0=\ker T_b\). For every \(c\),

\begin{equation} T_c(N_0)\subseteq U\cap U^{\perp _J}. \end{equation}
2

Orthogonality follows from (3) with \(x\in N_0\). For \(T_c(N_0)\subseteq U\), write \(T_b\) as an identity block of size \(s\) and zeros; the coefficient of \(t\) in an \((s+1)\)-minor of \(T_b+tT_c\) bordering that block is an entry of \(T_c\colon N_0\to E/U\), and maximality of \(s\) forces it to vanish.

If \(s=r{\lt}a\), then \(U=E\), \(U\cap U^{\perp _J}=0\), and (4) gives a common kernel \(N_0\ne 0\), a contradiction. If \(s=r=a\), \(T_b\) is invertible; complete \(b\) to a basis \(b,c,d\). The operators \(T_b^{-1}T_c\), \(T_b^{-1}T_d\) are self-adjoint for \(J(T_b\, \cdot ,T_b\, \cdot )\) by (3), and (3) for the pair \(c,d\) makes their product self-adjoint as well, so they commute; a common eigenvector \(x\ne 0\) gives \(T_cx,T_dx\in {\mathbb {C}}\, T_bx\), a line. If \(s{\lt}r\), put \(R=U\cap U^{\perp _J}\); then \(\dim N_0=a-s\ge r-s\ge \dim R\). If \(R=0\) there is again a common kernel. Otherwise \(T_c|_{N_0},T_d|_{N_0}\colon N_0\to R\) form a pencil: if \(\dim N_0{\gt}\dim R\) every member has a kernel, and if the dimensions agree the binary form \(\det (\sigma T_c+\tau T_d)|_{N_0}\) is zero or of positive degree, so it vanishes at some \([\sigma :\tau ]\in {\mathbb {P}}^1({\mathbb {C}})\). Either way there is \(x\in N_0\setminus 0\) with \(T_bx=0\) and \(T_cx,T_dx\) dependent, not both zero; they span a line. In all cases the first paragraph gives a contradiction.

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

1. A Hankel multiplier whose image avoids the simple tensors cannot be small: its rank is at least one more than the number \(a\) of \(x\) directions it actually uses. 2. Why: if \(r\le a\), linear algebra over \(\mathbb C\) produces a vector \(x\) whose three images \(T_{y}x\) lie on one line, and the Hankel symmetry turns that line into a simple tensor in the image. The three cases are: a common kernel; commuting self-adjoint operators with a common eigenvector; or a square pencil that is singular at some point of \(\mathbb P^1(\mathbb C)\). 3. With Corollary 4 this gives \(a+1\le r\le m\), the inequality that drives the induction; in [BDS] this place is taken by the classification of Gorenstein ideals. 4. A numerical search over Hankel matrices of rank at most four in size \(4\times 3\) found no counterexample, and found the case rank four, support three, no simple tensor, so the last case of the induction is not empty.

↳ Used in: Theorem 1

Proof ▼

The displayed expression has \(2m\) squares. Write any representation as

\[ F_m=\Big\| \sum _ix_iM_iy\Big\| ^2,\qquad M_i\in {\mathbb {R}}^{s\times 3}. \]

The coefficient of \(x_i^2\) gives \(M_i^\top M_i\) of rank two with kernel spanned by \(b_i=(1,i,0)\). For \(i\ne j\) the coefficient of \(x_ix_j\) vanishes, so \(M_i^\top M_j\) is antisymmetric; it kills \(b_j\) on the right and \(b_i\) on the left, hence, being antisymmetric, \(b_i\) and \(b_j\) on the right. An antisymmetric \(3\times 3\) matrix with two independent vectors in its kernel is zero. So the images of the \(M_i\) are \(m\) planes, pairwise orthogonal in \({\mathbb {R}}^s\), and \(s\ge 2m\).

Proof ▼

We prove more generally \({\mathrm{BSR}}(m,n)\le mn-\max (m,n)\) for \(m,n\ge 3\); for \(n=4\le m\) this is \(3m\). This is the argument of [ 7 , Theorem 9 ] with Theorem 1 in place of width two. Let \(m\ge n\) and \(f=\sum _{k\le r}b_k^2\); let \(v_{ij}\in {\mathbb {R}}^r\) be the coefficient vector of \(x_iy_j\) in \((b_1,\dots ,b_r)\). The vectors \(v_{ij}\) with \(j\ge 4\) span a space \(W\) of dimension at most \(m(n-3)\). After an orthogonal change of the \(r\) coordinates, which does not change \(f\), \(W\) lies in the first \(\dim W\) of them, and the remaining coordinates give bilinear forms in \(x\) and \(y_1,y_2,y_3\) only. Their sum of squares is an \(m\times 3\) biquadratic sum of squares, hence a sum of \(2m\) squares by Theorem 1. So \(f\) is a sum of \(m(n-3)+2m=mn-m\) squares. For \(m{\lt}n\) exchange the roles of \(x\) and \(y\). The lower bound \({\mathrm{BSR}}(4,4)\ge 11\) is [ 8 , Theorem 3.1 ] .

💡 In plain words (commentary, not part of the paper)

1. Every \(m\times 4\) biquadratic sum of squares is a sum of at most \(3m\) squares; for \(4\times 4\) this is \(12\), and Qi and Cui showed that \(11\) are sometimes needed, so \(11\le \mathrm{BSR}(4,4)\le 12\). 2. Why: in any representation, rotate the squares so that the coefficients of \(y_4\) live in at most \(m\) of them. The other squares do not see \(y_4\): they form an \(m\times 3\) sum of squares, which Theorem 1 rewrites with \(2m\) squares. Total \(m+2m=3m\). 3. The same rotation gives \(mn-\max (m,n)\) in any size. It is new only in width four: for width three it is Theorem 1 itself, and from width five on the bound of [BSSV, Theorem 2.1] is as good or better (for \(6\times 6\): \(29\) against \(30\)). The lab draws the three bounds. 4. The rotation is the one of our earlier note [Mar, Theorem 9], which used width two and gave \(3m+1\).
