📐 Setting (copied from the paper, §§1–2)

Let \(H\) be a real symmetric \(n\times n\) matrix with spectrum in \([-1,1]\), and

\[ P_H(x,y)=(x_1^2+x_2^2)\, |y|^2+2x_1x_2\, y^{\top }Hy,\qquad M(t)=(1+t^2)I+2tH. \]

For a subring \(R\subseteq \mathbb R\), \(\ell _R(H)\) is the least number of squares of bilinear forms with coefficients in \(R\) whose sum is \(P_H\). For a graph \(G\), \(\ell _R(G)=\ell _R(\tfrac 12A_G)\). We write \(H_n=(J-I)/(n-1)\) and \(\operatorname {sym}T=(T+T^{\top })/2\).

Abstract.

For a rational symmetric matrix \(H\) with spectrum in \([-1,1]\), how many squares of rational bilinear forms are needed to write \(P_H(x,y)=(x_1^2+x_2^2)|y|^2+2x_1x_2\, y^{\top }Hy\)? Over \({\mathbb {R}}\) the answer is \(n\) or \(n+1\) (Blekherman–Plaumann–Sinn–Vinzant, Hanselka–Sinn). Over \({\mathbb {Q}}\) it is the least dimension of a sum of squares carrying an orthogonal dilation of a contraction with symmetric part \(H\), and it is governed by arithmetic: a rotation with rational cosine \(\lambda \) needs its sine in \({\mathbb {Q}}(\sqrt{\lambda ^2-1})\), and the cost of supplying it is measured by the number of rational squares needed for \(\sin ^2\theta =1-\lambda ^2\). We give a criterion by an embedding over \({\mathbb {Q}}(t)\); the exact length for the normalized complete graphs \(H_n=(J-I)/(n-1)\), which is \(n\), \(n+3\) or \(n+5\) for odd \(n\) according to squares and \(n\) modulo \(8\), and \(n+\tau (s)\) for even \(n\); for every \(H\) with one interior eigenvalue, an exact finite local procedure built on Bayer-Fluckiger’s isometry criterion and the bound \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(H)-n\le 7\), which is attained; and the closed form \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(\lambda I_m)=m+\min \bigl((-m)\bmod 2s,\ \tau (1-\lambda ^2)\bigr)\), with \(s\) the level of \({\mathbb {Q}}(\sqrt{\lambda ^2-1})\). We also give the integer length of every graph and the smallest gaps between \({\mathbb {R}}\) and \({\mathbb {Q}}\).

1 Introduction

Let \(H\) be a real symmetric \(n\times n\) matrix with spectrum in \([-1,1]\). The form

\begin{equation} \tag {1} P_H(x,y)=(x_1^2+x_2^2)\, |y|^2+2x_1x_2\, y^{\top }Hy \end{equation}
1

is nonnegative. If \(H=\tfrac 12 A\) with \(A\) the adjacency matrix of a graph, then \(H\) is, up to an affine change, the Hückel matrix of the graph. Since \(x\) has two variables, \(P_H\) is a sum of squares of bilinear forms, and Blekherman, Plaumann, Sinn and Vinzant [ 8 ] and Hanselka and Sinn [ 20 ] give the real length: at most \(n+1\), and \(n\) exactly when \(\det M(t)\) is a square, where \(M(t)=(1+t^2)I+2tH\).

We ask what happens to this length when the coefficients are restricted. Comparing degrees shows that a representation of \(M(t)=P^{\top }P\) over \(R[t]\) has \(P\) of degree at most one, so the length below is the length \(\ell (M(t))\) of Fernando, Ruiz and Scheiderer [ 16 ] , whose theorem gives \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(H)\le 8n\). In another language, \(\ell _K(H)\) is the least dimension of a standard space \(K^r\) carrying an orthogonal one-step dilation of a contraction \(T\) with \({\operatorname {sym}}T=H\). Over \({\mathbb {R}}\) a contraction \(T\) has a dilation of dimension \(n+\operatorname{rank}(I-T^{\top }T)\) [ 23 ] . Over \({\mathbb {Q}}\) the ambient space must be a sum of unit squares, and this is where arithmetic enters.

The picture. Over \({\mathbb {R}}\), the length is \(n\) when \(H\) is the symmetric part of an orthogonal matrix, which is a sum of plane rotations: an interior eigenvalue \(\lambda =\cos \theta \) is the cosine of a rotation, whose sine \(\sqrt{1-\lambda ^2}\) always exists. Over \({\mathbb {Q}}\) the rotation of cosine \(\lambda \) lives on a rational plane only if its sine is supplied, by the field \(L={\mathbb {Q}}(\sqrt{\lambda ^2-1})\); what is missing has to be completed by extra rational squares. For \(H=\frac34 I_2\) the real length is \(2\), a rotation by \(\cos \theta =\frac34\), \(\sin \theta =\frac{\sqrt7}4\). Over \({\mathbb {Q}}\) the sine is not available, \(7\) needs four rational squares, and the length is \(6\): with

\[ A=\binom {I_2}{0},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix} \frac34 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac34 \\ \frac12 & -\frac14 \\ \frac14 & \frac12 \\ \frac14 & \frac14 \\ \frac14 & -\frac14 \end{pmatrix}\in {\mathbb {Q}}^{6\times 2}, \]

one has \(A^{\top }A=B^{\top }B=I\) and \(A^{\top }B+B^{\top }A=2H\), the last four rows of \(B\) being the first two columns of the left multiplication by the quaternion \(\frac14(2+i+j+k)\), of norm \(\frac7{16}\); Theorem 6.15 shows that \(6\) is optimal.

Where the numbers come from. Four mechanisms account for every number in the paper.

  1. The sine. A rotation of cosine \(\lambda \) exists on a rational plane exactly when the plane is \(\langle a,a\delta \rangle \) with \(\delta =1-\lambda ^2=\sin ^2\theta \), up to isometry: the plane must carry the sine. So length \(n\) means that each interior eigenspace splits into such planes, and this is a hyperbolicity condition over \(L={\mathbb {Q}}(\sqrt{\lambda ^2-1})\) (Theorem 5.1).

  2. The complement. When it fails, what is missing must be supplied inside a sum of squares: the extra squares are the dimension of a positive rational form \(p\subseteq I_h\) such that the eigenspace form together with \(p\) splits (Theorem 6.2). A positive rational form of dimension \(d\) needs at most \(d+3\) squares, by the four-square theorem and Hasse–Minkowski (Lemma 6.1); this is the \(3\) in the bounds \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}\le n+d_{\mathbb {Q}}+3\), and with \(d_{\mathbb {Q}}\le 4\) it gives the \(7\) of Corollary 6.8.

  3. The split primes. Locally, the condition is felt only where \(L\) splits: there \(L\otimes {\mathbb {Q}}_p={\mathbb {Q}}_p\times {\mathbb {Q}}_p\), so splitting over \(L\) means splitting over \({\mathbb {Q}}_p\), and a local sign (a Hasse invariant) must match; at the other primes the local conditions can be met by choosing \(p\) (Theorem 6.7). This is why every failure of the determinant bound that we found comes from a split prime (Example 6.16).

  4. The level. The least number \(s\) of squares with sum \(-1\) in \(L\) is \(1\), \(2\) or \(4\), and \(I_{2s}\) is the first sum of squares that becomes hyperbolic over \(L\); so \(\langle 1\rangle \) has order \(2s\) in the Witt group of \(L\), and \(2s\) divides \(8\), the period that holds for every such \(L\). For \(\lambda =\frac34\), \(L={\mathbb {Q}}(\sqrt{-7})\) has \(s=4\), so \(I_8\) is the first one: this is the \(8\) of the period (Theorem 4.2) and of the general bound \(8n\) of [ 16 ] . The level is read off from \(\tau (\delta )\), the number of rational squares needed for \(\sin ^2\theta \) (Lemma 6.14), and this is the \(\tau \) in the scalar formula.

For the complete graphs the only interior eigenvalue is \(\lambda =-1/(n-1)\), with \(\delta =n(n-2)/(n-1)^2\), so \(\delta \) is \(n(n-2)\) up to squares, and its eigenspace \(\mathbf1^{\perp }\subseteq I_n\) has determinant \(n\); this is why squares among \(n\) and \(n-2\), and the residues of \(n\) modulo \(8\), decide \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(H_n)\).

Main results.

  1. A criterion for length and defect by an embedding over \({\mathbb {Q}}(t)\) (Theorem 4.1), period \(8\) (Theorem 4.2), and a decision procedure for \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}\le n+1\) (Theorem 5.5).

  2. For every \(H\) with one interior eigenvalue, the reduction of the length to a positive complement inside a sum of squares (Theorem 6.2), an exact finite local procedure (Theorem 6.7) and the bound \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(H)-n\le 7\) (Corollary 6.8), attained by an explicit \(6\times 6\) matrix, the least order possible, with an exact local criterion in multiplicity \(4\) (Theorem 6.9).

  3. For scalar matrices, \(\ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(\lambda I_m)\) in closed form, decided by \(\tau (1-\lambda ^2)\) through the level of \(L\) (Theorem 6.15).

  4. For the normalized complete graphs, length and defect for every \(n\) (Theorem 7.1).

We also give the integer length of every graph (Theorem 8.1), the smallest examples of a gap between \({\mathbb {R}}\) and \({\mathbb {Q}}\) (Theorem 9.1, Proposition 4.4), components that interact and a defect of \(7\) (Propositions 4.5, 4.6), and fields of definition (Section 10). Sums of squares with more variables \(x\) are treated in a separate note.

What is known and what is new. We have tried to credit everything that is not ours.

  • The real length is due to Blekherman, Plaumann, Sinn and Vinzant [ 8 ] and Hanselka and Sinn [ 20 ] ; Corollary 5.4 is a short consequence of [ 20 , Lemma 4.3, Corollaries 2.7 and 4.5 ] and Witt cancellation.

  • The criterion for length \(n\) (Theorem 5.1) is close to the classical theory of isometries and hermitian forms: Milnor [ 31 ] , Milnor–Husemoller [ 29 , Appendix 2 ] , Lewis [ 24 ] , and Bayer-Fluckiger [ 4 , Theorem 9.1, Corollary 9.2 ] , whose criterion is also the input of Section 6; what is ours there is the constraint that the complement lies in a sum of squares, the finite procedure and the bound.

  • In Theorem 6.15, the case \(2s\mid m\) is a special case of Fernando–Ruiz–Scheiderer [ 16 , Proposition 1.7 ] , and \(m=1\) is elementary, in the spirit of Pourchet [ 27 ] ; we have not found the general formula.

  • For Theorem 7.1, the case where \(n-2\) is a square is the construction of Bailey and Craigen from doubly regular tournaments [ 1 , Theorem 4.2 ] ; \(\pm 1\) matrices of this shape are conference matrices [ 19 , 14 ] , rational orthogonal matrices with zero pattern \(J-I\) appear in [ 13 , Proposition 12 ] , and regular rational orthogonal matrices are parametrized in [ 36 , Theorem 4 ] . None of these determines when a rational orthogonal matrix, of size \(n\) or larger, has symmetric part \((J-I)/(n-1)\).

  • Theorem 8.1 follows from the coefficient comparison of Yiu [ 38 , § 1 ] ; the four real squares of Theorem 9.1 are known (Remark 9.2).

  • The context is the line of Bender [ 7 ] , Bass–Estes–Guralnick [ 2 ] , Friedland [ 17 ] and Bayer-Fluckiger–Berhuy–Chuard-Koulmann [ 3 ] on rational symmetric and orthogonal matrices; over \(\mathbb C\), de la Cruz and Paras [ 12 ] describe \(X+X^{\top }\) with \(X\) orthogonal.

We could not consult Pfister’s and Cassels’ books on multiples of forms, nor Friedland [ 17 ] and Bass–Estes–Guralnick [ 2 ] in full, where special cases of Theorems 6.15 and 7.1 might appear. Not having found a result is not a proof of novelty.

Proposition 2.1

Huckel_SOS.represents_iff Let \(K\subseteq {\mathbb {R}}\) be a field, \(H\) symmetric over \(K\) of size \(n\ge 1\), and \(r\ge n\). The following are equivalent:

  1. \(\ell _K(H)\le r\);

  2. there are \(A,B\in K^{r\times n}\) with \(A^{\top }A=B^{\top }B=I\) and \(A^{\top }B+B^{\top }A=2H\);

  3. there is \(T\in K^{n\times n}\) with \({\operatorname {sym}}T=H\) such that \(I-T^{\top }T=D^{\top }D\) for some \(D\in K^{(r-n)\times n}\).

In particular \(\ell _K(H)=n\) if and only if \(H={\operatorname {sym}}T\) with \(T\in O_n(K)\).

Proof ▼

A sum of \(r\) squares is \(|x_1Ay+x_2By|^2\), and comparing coefficients in (1) gives (2). Given (2), the space \(K^r\) with the standard form is the orthogonal sum of \(A(K^n)\cong n\langle 1\rangle \) and a complement which, by Witt cancellation, is isometric to \((r-n)\langle 1\rangle \). So we may take \(A=\binom {I}{0}\) and \(B=\binom {T}{D}\). The converse is the same computation.

Definition 2.2
#
📕 p. 5📍 map

The defect \(d_K(H)\) is the least rank of \(I-T^{\top }T\) over \(T\in K^{n\times n}\) with \({\operatorname {sym}}T=H\) and \(T^{\top }T\le I\).

↳ Not used by another result of the paper.

The matrix \(\mathcal G=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} I & T \\ T^{\top } & I \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) is a Gram matrix of \(P_H\) on the products \(x_iy_j\), and its rank is \(n+\operatorname{rank}(I-T^{\top }T)\) by the Schur complement. So \(n+d_{\mathbb {Q}}(H)\) is the least rank of a rational positive semidefinite Gram matrix, and \(d_{\mathbb {Q}}(H)\le \ell _{\mathbb {Q}}(H)-n\). Over \({\mathbb {R}}\) the two agree. Over \({\mathbb {Q}}\) they do not, because a rational form of rank \(d\) need not be a sum of \(d\) rational squares.

Huckel_SOS.blocks_orthogonal, Huckel_SOS.blocks_symmetric_part Let \(H\) have spectrum in \([-1,1]\).

  1. \(\ell _{\mathbb {R}}(H)=n\) if every eigenvalue in \((-1,1)\) has even multiplicity, and \(\ell _{\mathbb {R}}(H)=n+1\) otherwise.

  2. Let \(Q\in O_{n+1}({\mathbb {R}})\) with \({\operatorname {sym}}Q=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} H & b \\ b^{\top } & c \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), and let \(\lambda \in (-1,1)\) have even multiplicity \(m\) in \(H\). Then \(b\) is orthogonal to the eigenspace \(E_\lambda \), and \(Q\) preserves it.

🆕 Classical and new: the length is due to Blekherman–Plaumann–Sinn–Vinzant and Hanselka–Sinn; the proof and the splitting are ours

↳ Used in: Proposition 3.3, Proposition 4.4, Corollary 5.4

Proof ▼

A real orthogonal matrix is a sum of plane rotations and \(\pm 1\), and a rotation of angle \(\theta \) gives \(\cos \theta \) twice in \({\operatorname {sym}}Q\). With Proposition 2.1, \(r=n\) forces parity, and conversely a parity spectrum is realized by rotation blocks.

Remove the even part and \(\pm 1\). There remain simple interior values \(\lambda _1{\gt}\dots {\gt}\lambda _s\). If \(s\) is odd take \(\mu =(\lambda _1,\lambda _1,\lambda _3,\lambda _3,\dots ,\lambda _s,\lambda _s)\), and if \(s\) is even take \(\mu =(1,\lambda _2,\lambda _2,\dots ,\lambda _s,\lambda _s)\). These \(s+1\) values interlace the \(\lambda _i\), \(\mu _1\ge \lambda _1\ge \mu _2\ge \dots \ge \lambda _s\ge \mu _{s+1}\), so by Fan–Pall [ 15 ] there is a symmetric matrix with spectrum \(\mu \) containing \(\operatorname {diag}(\lambda _i)\) as a principal block. Explicitly, the bordered matrix \(S=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \operatorname {diag}(\lambda ) & b \\ b^{\top } & c \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) has \(\det (x-S)=(x-c)\prod _k(x-\lambda _k)-\sum _ib_i^2\prod _{k\ne i}(x-\lambda _k)\), so it suffices to take \(c=\sum \mu _j-\sum \lambda _i\) and \(b_i^2=-\prod _j(\lambda _i-\mu _j)/\prod _{k\ne i}(\lambda _i-\lambda _k)\), which is \(\ge 0\) by interlacing, with equality exactly when \(\lambda _i\) is one of the \(\mu _j\). The interior eigenvalues of \(S\) are paired and the remaining one, if any, is \(1\), so \(S={\operatorname {sym}}Q\) for an orthogonal \(Q\) of order \(s+1\) made of rotation blocks in an eigenbasis of \(S\); the direct sum with the removed part gives \({\operatorname {sym}}\) of an orthogonal matrix of order \(n+1\).

For (2): if \(b\not\perp E_\lambda \) then \(\lambda \) has multiplicity exactly \(m-1\) in \({\operatorname {sym}}Q\), which is odd. So \(b\perp E_\lambda \); the multiplicity is then \(m\), as \(m+1\) is also odd, and \(E_\lambda \) is an eigenspace of \({\operatorname {sym}}Q\), with which \(Q\) commutes.

🆕 Classical and new: applies the Harder and Gerstein lattice argument to the normalized pencil

↳ Used in: Theorem 4.2, Proposition 4.4, 6.16

Proof ▼

Let \(Q_0=I_n\perp p\), of dimension \(N\), write \(R={\mathbb {Q}}[t]\) and let \(\Lambda _0=R^N\) be its constant lattice, which is unimodular. A lattice is a finitely generated \(R\)-submodule of \(F^N\) of rank \(N\), and it is integral if the form takes values in \(R\) on it. Suppose \(v_1,\dots ,v_n\in F^N\) have Gram matrix \(M_H\).

Step 1: a maximal lattice. Since \(\det M_H\ne 0\), the \(v_i\) span a nondegenerate subspace; adding a basis of its orthogonal complement, scaled to make the Gram matrix polynomial, gives an integral lattice \(N_0\ni v_1,\dots ,v_n\). Every integral lattice \(L\supseteq N_0\) lies in the dual lattice \(N_0^{\# }\), a finitely generated module over the noetherian ring \(R\), so there is a maximal integral lattice \(L\supseteq N_0\).

Step 2: its local shape. Let \(\pi \) be a prime of \(R\), \(k_\pi =R/\pi \) and \(L_\pi \) the completion of \(L\) over the completed ring \(\hat R_\pi \). As \(2\) is a unit, \(L_\pi \) is diagonalizable, so \(L_\pi =U\perp \pi V\perp \pi ^2W\) with \(U,V,W\) unimodular (the last summand collecting all valuations \(\ge 2\)). An integral lattice can be changed at one prime and kept at the others: if \(L''\supsetneq L_\pi \) is an integral \(\hat R_\pi \)-lattice with \(L''\subseteq \pi ^{-k}L_\pi \), then \(L'=\{ x\in \pi ^{-k}L: x\in L''\} \) is an \(R\)-lattice with \(L'_\pi =L''\) and \(L'_\rho =L_\rho \) for \(\rho \ne \pi \), as \(\pi \) is a unit at \(\rho \); it is integral because integrality is checked prime by prime, \(R=\bigcap _\rho R_{(\rho )}\). (This is the usual local–global correspondence for lattices over a Dedekind domain [ 26 ] ; \(L'\) is a global lattice by construction, not merely a completed one.) So maximality of \(L\) gives maximality of \(L_\pi \). If \(W\ne 0\), replacing \(\pi ^2W\) by \(\pi W\) keeps the lattice integral, so \(W=0\). If \(\bar V\) is isotropic, take \(v\in V\) primitive with \(b_V(v,v)\in \pi \hat R_\pi \), where \(b_V\) is the unimodular form of \(V\), so that the vector \(x=v\) of the summand \(\pi V\) has norm \(\pi b_V(v,v)\in \pi ^2\hat R_\pi \). Then \(x/\pi \) has norm \(b_V(v,v)/\pi \in \hat R_\pi \), it is orthogonal to \(U\), and its product with \(w\in \pi V\) is \(b_V(v,w)\in \hat R_\pi \). So \(L_\pi +\hat R_\pi \, x/\pi \) is integral, and strictly larger since \(v\) is primitive. So \(\bar V\) is anisotropic.

Step 3: \(V=0\). By Springer’s theorem over the complete field \(F_\pi \) [ 21 , Ch. VI ] , the second residue of \(L_\pi \otimes F_\pi \) is the class \([\bar V]\in W(k_\pi )\), and it depends only on the space. The same space contains the unimodular lattice \(\Lambda _0\), whose second residue is \(0\). So \([\bar V]=0\), and since \(\bar V\) is anisotropic, \(\bar V=0\): an anisotropic form with zero Witt class has dimension zero. Hence \(V=0\) and \(L\) is unimodular at every prime, that is, unimodular over \(R\). (Metabolic residues cannot occur, because maximality has already made \(\bar V\) anisotropic.)

Step 4: \(L\) is constant. By the theorem of Harder and Djoković, in the form [ 16 , Theorem 1.11 ] , a symmetric matrix in \(\mathrm{GL}_N(R)\) is congruent over \(R\) to a matrix with coefficients in \({\mathbb {Q}}\). So \(L\) has an \(R\)-basis with constant Gram matrix \(Q_1\). The constant forms \(Q_0,Q_1\) are isometric over \(F\); specializing an isometry at a rational \(t_0\) where it and its inverse are defined shows \(Q_1\cong Q_0\) over \({\mathbb {Q}}\). Composing, there is an isometry \(\varphi \) of \((Q_0)_F\) with \(\varphi (L)=\Lambda _0\), and the vectors \(\varphi (v_i)\) are polynomial.

Step 5: degree one. In a rational basis diagonalizing \(Q_0\) with positive coefficients \(c_k\), the norm \(\sum _kc_kw_k(t)^2\) of a polynomial vector \(w\ne 0\) has degree \(2\max _k\deg w_k\), since the leading terms cannot cancel. The diagonal of \(M_H\) has degree two, so the matrix of the \(\varphi (v_i)\) is \(A_0+tB_0\), and comparing coefficients in \((1+t^2)I+2tH\) gives \(A_0^*A_0=B_0^*B_0=I\) and \(A_0^*B_0+B_0^*A_0=2H\), with \({}^*\) the adjoint for \(Q_0\). By Witt’s theorem \(A_0\) may be taken to be the inclusion of \(I_n\); then \(B_0=\binom {T}{D}\) with \({\operatorname {sym}}T=H\) and \(I-T^{\top }T=D^*D\), whose nondegenerate part is the restriction of \(p\) to the image of \(D\), so it embeds in \(p\). This is (1). Conversely, given (1), an embedding of the defect form in \(p\) gives \(D\) with \(D^*D=I-T^{\top }T\), and the columns of \(\binom {I}{0}+t\binom {T}{D}\) have Gram matrix \(M_H\). For the length take \(p=I_{r-n}\): a polynomial matrix of degree one with Gram matrix \(M_H\) in \(I_r\) is a representation of \(P_H\) by \(r\) squares.

Proof ▼

As in Yiu [ 38 , § 1 ] , an integer column of norm one is \(\pm e_k\). So the columns of \(A\) and \(B\) are signed coordinate vectors, at distinct coordinates by orthogonality, and \(T=A^{\top }B\) is a signed partial permutation matrix: each row and column has at most one nonzero entry. Thus each row of \(T+T^{\top }=A_G\) has at most two nonzero entries.

Moreover \(r=2n-\operatorname {nnz}(T)\) for the coordinates actually used. Each edge needs exactly one nonzero entry among \(T_{ij},T_{ji}\). A pair \(T_{ij}=-T_{ji}\ne 0\) on a non-edge uses a free row and a free column at both vertices, which only isolated vertices have. So \(\operatorname {nnz}(T)\le |E|+2\lfloor z/2\rfloor \), where \(z\) is the number of isolated vertices. Equality is attained: orient each cycle and each path, so that each edge carries one entry, and pair the isolated vertices by quarter turns; unused indices of the two families go to distinct coordinates. Since \(|E|\) is \(n\) minus the number of path components, isolated vertices included, this gives the formula.
